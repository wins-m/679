It’s already mid-December.

I’ve heard people say: once you start working, your sense of the years passing gradually fades. We no longer get to use Grade to remember our age, or use the year we started school and the year we graduated (the numbers you’re always asked to fill in on forms) to recall the people and things around those years — I haven’t had that experience yet.

Last year, on New Year’s Eve, at a friend’s place by the river: the three of us — one who had just arrived, one who’d been here a long while, one about to leave. We ordered delivery, watched Once Upon a Time in America and 2001: A Space Odyssey (the usual ritual), split most of a bottle of gin between a bottle of Kirin and tonic water, and took a cab to go clubbing by West Lake. We never got in.

To catch a cab home, we walked toward West Lake. This old-town district, at the turn of the year, was peacefully quiet. “Tucked away in a corner of the city, right by the lake — settling down here wouldn’t be bad either,” was more or less a way of talking about the ultimate ideal of hiding in plain sight within the city.

Winter. Later on I’d often run along the river from Aoti to Binjiang, and as the scenery on the far bank shifted from a modern CBD to mountains, lakes, and the outskirts, and I’d marvel at how beautiful the mountain scenery was, I’d always think, “does this even count? At this rate West Lake might as well ascend to immortality.”

I don’t go to West Lake often. You could say I don’t go at all. Half an hour’s distance already feels too far, and leaving the city is even more unnecessary. A week of internship only requires me on-site three or four days, and the rest of the time I curl up at home; I go out once a day, to the riverside — following the momentum that carried on from those three years. If it’s a weekday, all the better: the afternoon sun around three or four, the riverside park completely empty; on the rare weekend, I get to watch, from a distance, young families and young people camping and skateboarding. Even needing to work only three days a week, the days still felt full.

After those three years ended, and after the failed attempt to make them continue, I no longer seem to be chasing the life of a golden age — the kind evoked by “Beijing Welcomes You,” “that soaring era.” The habits formed as a student would carry on year after year even afterward. Before Spring Festival, out of habit, I’d worry about running out of food and stock up on instant meals from JD, only to find that delivery was never remotely close to running out, and it wasn’t until I moved that I barely managed to use them all up.

I haven’t updated social media in a long time — long stretches with no trace at all. Last August I digitized my movie ticket stubs with Hugo, and later that year switched to a new template for the site — you can clearly see how sparse the number of ticket stubs was in those years. Actually, using a timeline plus movie ticket stubs is a convenient way to look back: not only can you recall what you were doing and where, and what you had in your stomach when you watched a particular film (usually on a weekend), but in the gaps between viewings you can also notice the long stretches of absence — and the absent, forgotten memories. What gets remembered is decided by self-awareness; what gets overlooked is decided by the real self.

It even snowed in February. I didn’t go out all winter, relying entirely on the floor-to-ceiling windows on the high floor — the high-rises outside, the park, the river and its bridges, the distant mountains, and the sun hanging high above. The snow fell heavily, like goose feathers, bright and distinct under the strong lights (the decorative lighting meant to be seen from the opposite bank). It would drift for a long while before landing. And the region south of the Yangtze needs several rounds of snowfall before it can accumulate into a real snow cover. Though, by that point, the accumulated snow had nothing to do with someone who never left the house.

This was before Spring Festival. Because after Spring Festival, the days would once again dissolve into forgetfulness. Over the holiday I played White Album nonstop, though in the end I never made it all the way through any single main route.

In March, all I knew was that the weather was turning warmer, and the park below my company’s office was gradually turning green. I came to depend on strolling through the park during lunch break, to the point that the days I didn’t go to the office became hard to get through. So I got into the habit of walking to McDonald’s for breakfast after getting up, listening to Studio Classroom along the way, or in the afternoon going to a nearby neighborhood park to sit in the sun before heading into a cinema.

The cinema membership card had a weekly special every Thursday, and conveniently there were new imported films to watch every week around then. Daytime Beauty, Nighttime Beast, Anatomy of a Fall, Brief History of a Family — and just like that, time arrived at April. I remember taking the subway to the nearest Wanda to watch Borrowed Time (and Kim’s Video) — going to the cinema during work-from-home hours, feeling a bit guilty about it in my heart. After the film I biked a shared bike back home along the river, golden light spilling over the whole way.

So actually, the McDonald’s routine and the return to the cinema were both April things. In April, I took a week off to write my thesis, then two weeks, and it eventually stretched into a whole month. An experiment I’d finished in a week left me sinking into endless self-doubt by the time I wrote up the first draft. I lay in bed all night watching Harry Potter, and after finishing all eight films, kept going with a Kindle to read the novels. The weather kept getting hotter, and the room with its south-facing floor-to-ceiling windows already called for summer clothes.

I finally finished revising the thesis. The experiments were complete, the results stable and significant — it’s just that most of the writing was done bleary-eyed through the night, then polished with GPT. It wasn’t perfect, but among the many imperfect parts I’d already lost track of which ones actually needed fixing most. “That’ll do” — as a professional master’s thesis at my not-so-prestigious school, it was already limited-but-good-enough.

In May I was back at my desk. I only took one day off to go to Beijing for the thesis defense, then extended it to three days (essentially a full week). The thesis, as expected, held up without a hitch, and I regained a bit of confidence — except this kind of situation, one that could be controlled purely by personal ability, has all but vanished from life.

I slept in the day after the defense, then went to CAFA to see the graduation exhibition. I skipped my cohort’s graduation activities. I already knew I wouldn’t be attending this master’s degree commencement ceremony. I went to Xicheng to finish the tripartite agreement, then waited at a solemn, dignified little Chaoshan beef restaurant by the road near Xuanwumen for a former colleague to get off work — I’d decided to stay in Beijing that week. An evening at a video rental place in the south watching The Piano Teacher; an evening wandering Sanlitun’s Sanlian Bookstore and eating sour beef hotpot; an afternoon wandering around Yonghegong, Dongjiaominxiang, and Yangmeizhu Xieji; a morning waking up at Changchunyuan; a small hours eating fried noodles at Jie Ge’s. I watched A Time to Live, A Time to Die in the basement of Yuanpei, but I had no memory of it at all until I was recently sorting through my hard drive and found I needed to move it from my “want to watch” folder into “watched — Chinese-language.”

Looking back now, that week off was almost too dazzling — probably entirely because of the confidence restored after the successful defense. Back at my Sisyphean desk, I hesitated a long while before proposing to take June off to travel, until I officially graduated and started working full-time.

Taking time off was just an excuse; the real trip had been building up momentum, just without a destination. I rested at home for a week, watched for a fair flight price, and flew to Xishuangbanna.

Banna, before the rainy season arrived, seemed not so different from the region south of the Yangtze — if you don’t want to go to the scenic spots, and don’t have the resolve to head deep into the jungle. I went from Gaozhuang to downtown Jinghong and back to Gaozhuang, and sudden downpours kept disrupting my plans to go out, until finally I was forced to plan a new trip just to make myself value the chance to explore Banna. Once I’d gotten my train ticket, I chose to leave the safe world of the resort hotel, go to a bank and the market to get local currency and gear, check into a youth hostel (to look for travel companions), then head to a nearby mall for the thing I most needed — a movie (The Imaginary), passing on the way a school I’d have gone to as a kid heading in after a nap; trying the dark, mysterious marinated dishes at a roadside rice-noodle stall; and after the film, eating salt-pickled mango and octopus.

The new trip came from the cross-border high-speed rail that opened this year. You can buy a ticket straight to Laos on 12306 — this is information you’d only get pushed to you while in Banna. Walking through immigration after getting off the train, I finally felt an excitement I hadn’t felt in a long time (“like an earthquake — from then on…”). My heart was pounding wildly: from the learned helplessness accumulated over the past few years, from the anxiety that had built up over many days facing a trip I hadn’t carefully prepared for, from the fact that this was the first time in my life I was crossing a national border alone. And all of it came from stumbling, in idle boredom in Banna, onto the overseas stations available on 12306.

In Luang Prabang, everything was gold. It was a sky with no power lines cutting across it, a landscape like the one painted on the Vietnamese coffee jars my father brought home years ago, a paradise on earth that neither Amap nor Google Maps could trace. Heading north against the current of the Mekong, the slow boat stopped at village after unnamed village, dropping off locals; before entering Thailand, the civilized world seemed like a distant abstraction.

The civilized world — a concept I’ve grown tired of. Some say Southeast Asia backpackers should start from Singapore and head north, so the pace of the journey goes from urgent to unhurried. In this fast-paced modern world, even wanderers can’t help but be affected. Chiang Rai’s transportation and attractions are thoroughly contemporary; Chiang Mai already has fully developed malls and cinemas, luxury shopping streets, universities, sprawling clubs and red-light districts. The hostel was plastered with dozens of flyers for Muay Thai, cooking classes, jungle trekking, zip-lining, and more, dizzying to look at; I ended up relying on museums and cinemas, and even went to a wildlife park — by way of a motorbike taxi under the blazing sun.

On the train to Bangkok, my seatmate was an undergraduate traveling to Chiang Mai alone (a cross-province trip). We couldn’t communicate in speech at all, but through a translation app I worked out that he was, like the protagonist of The Dancing Girl of Izu, traveling alone during his break. Most of the time he sat quietly by the window reading an English novel. Able to read fluently, yet unable to talk to the person in front of him — that might be who I was five years ago, and who I’ve been for the past five years.

A provincial-city version of Beijing. I’ve grown to like visiting universities more and more — their libraries, decentralized contemporary art museums, and cinemas. Right outside Chulalongkorn University was a film archive, and the screening happened to be some political party’s propaganda film; the leather sofas were empty except for me, in a thin jacket, freezing. In Bangkok I stayed right by the palace, never went to the commercial district — very much in keeping with someone who hasn’t been to Sanlitun in five years.

The last night, I took a ferry across the river to a dock to eat river prawns. River breeze and night, every table full of locals gathered for the weekend; before leaving, I finally got to see a bit of the country’s own people, beyond religion, royal power, and international culture.

Come July, the itinerary grew rushed. Whether to keep going by rail no longer mattered — what I was determined to do was reach the end of the line of the greater Southeast Asian railway. The Englishman on the tin-roofed train from Bangkok to the border; the ethnic Chinese descendants in Penang; the young man from Jiangsu-Zhejiang who flew in specially for durian; the Cantonese-only-speaking Hong Kong couple; the film-buff Indian auntie; in the Kuala Lumpur hostel, a businessman on a work trip, a diving enthusiast, a Saudi student; in Malacca, a young Englishman and the narrator known as “Brother De”; the old man at the Singapore hostel. Penang’s color is blue, Malaysia’s ethnic-heritage cities are green, but blue and green are the same color; photos remember the scenery, but what you remember of the journey are the people you met.

At NUS, watching from a distance a few clusters of master’s students taking their graduation photos, I finally got the graduation I actually wanted to see.

Scoot’s flight must have Fujianese shareholders — the plane specifically flew over Xiamen and Quanzhou. Lucky enough to have two seats to myself, I could just make out, far across the water, a stretch of what could no longer be called an island but a continent. I’ll be a southern Chinese, an Asian; the one thing I won’t do again is set foot in Beijing, won’t again regard the distant northern orthodoxy as some “mandate of heaven.”

Right after starting the new job, the Miwa Nishikawa film festival came around; I hadn’t expected that after coming back from the tropics I’d get heatstroke in the middle of a Beijing-like heat wave. Right after that, a company team-building trip to Guizhou — the tour-group format felt like being back in middle school or earlier. At the end of August I also went to Hangzhou Zhi Wing twice to see young directors’ work. After that, I lost interest in the hollow cultural events outside Beijing, and by year’s end hadn’t gone into a public cinema again.

Time then just flowed entirely from one weekend to the next, disappearing somewhere within the monthly “durable depreciable goods” budget allocation. One month spent tinkering with GPT for the NAS and smart home setup, one month building a PC to run Nintendo emulators, one month on road cycling, one month shuttling between the Dukoo universe and the Danread universe. I’ve now checked off, one by one, the “necessities” I used to swear during school were absolutely essential — except getting a cat. These are necessities, so whether I choose them or not is only about defending them — ever since losing them right when I was supposed to have them. Except, once you actually have a life, do you still need to fear you can’t sustain it?

That’s really the whole question. LD and HN were, over several months, my main targets of fierce criticism in the “PhD applicants” group made up of high school classmates. I think the standard for measuring a good life is fairly simple, but there’s a powerful top-down force driving people my age to feel restless about the present they could already have, to overdraw the present in pursuit of a future that’s “not up to them” (LD); and at the same time, given generational differences, they can choose to strike a deal with the time of the previous generations, which spreads out into a cyclone that the surrounding environment can’t avoid, making the situation worse for everyone their age (HN). At work, whenever I’d encounter related events or opinions, I’d fiercely criticize them in the group chat: this amounts to a stage performance that needs no audience, a way of expressing, through performance, emotions that reality won’t let show.

The friend who rang in the new year with me back at the start of it quit their job to focus on grad school applications, and almost everyone I know my age is now temporarily living in Shanghai. Starting in November, I traveled frequently between the two cities to handle various paperwork, and dropped in on “PhD applicants” gatherings along the way. Every meal I had in Shanghai, I ran into some new old friend, but I still couldn’t face this city alone before falling asleep — just like in Hangzhou, where I have every life necessity in place, I’ve never once stopped seeing it as a temporary resting spot.

And now, in the blink of an eye, it’s December, another winter solstice come round again, the same floor-to-ceiling window. As the years all turn into “once again,” a small, temporary illusion of safety gets added on top. Amid all kinds of news, the uncertainty of the future keeps announcing new historical highs; but the sunbathing and printed type in front of the floor-to-ceiling window on weekend afternoons is a certainty, and clocking out on time on weekdays to go exercise is a certainty. Whatever happens, this is what I’ve sought my “whole life” for: that line from Dominique in The Fountainhead — to want nothing, to expect nothing, to depend on nothing —

Freedom. If NetEase Cloud Music’s year-end keywords are “gained” and “freedom,” and the song of the year is Get Free, then why not?
