Twenty Years of Coincidence, Not Twenty Years of Oppression

26.9.1 5-minute read
WM
Podcast • Family • Freedom

30-second summary: In episode 6 of the Chinese podcast et Voice, Lumi describes leaving a conventional track to become a full-time polar expedition guide. What’s worth writing down isn’t “quit her job, went to Antarctica.” It’s four counterintuitive judgments: her predicament wasn’t oppression but alignment concealing the absence of self-direction; her sense of agency wasn’t reasoned into existence but purchased with a job opportunity she lost permanently; the criterion that turns “fleeing” into “departing” isn’t a healed wound but having a next destination — her second conflict was worse, and she was calmer; and the premise she named her own podcast after, the search for a “good place,” she ended up falsifying herself.

I recently listened to a podcast episode.1 The guest, Lumi, grew up in Ningbo, studied at the University of Hong Kong, and now works full-time as a polar expedition guide — sailing with a Norwegian cruise company, breaking trail for passengers at landing sites, piloting a Zodiac among the glaciers when the weather rules out landing, lecturing on penguins and whales during the crossing. Her own description: “sort of like an NPC in Antarctica.”

This kind of story gets written up as “escaped the track, went to see the world,” and then nothing further. But there are a few judgments in this episode considerably more precise than that genre, and I want to record them.

1. Her diagnosis beats the standard “East Asian kid” narrative Link to heading

The popular version: parents apply pressure, child complies, child later awakens and rebels.

Lumi’s material doesn’t support that version, and she dismantles it herself first. She says she was never an obedient kid — several relationships in high school, ranked around 100th out of 700-odd, not among the hardest workers. Her family ran a business, were busy, held no especially strong expectations about grades, and had no time to micromanage her. The pressure-and-compliance dynamic basically didn’t exist in her upbringing.

Her own diagnosis is far more precise:

Maybe I was never trying to satisfy them. I just happened to give them the result they wanted.

The weight of that sentence is that it describes not a history of oppression but a coincidence lasting twenty years. She went her own way; the output happened to land inside the range her family approved of; and so neither side was ever forced to examine what their standards actually were. The system ran beautifully — because it was never loaded.

This explains something else she says: when the conflict first erupted she was deeply confused, with “no idea what was happening to me,” only that they kept fighting. Under sustained oppression she would have had an interpretive framework ready. Precisely because it was a coincidence breaking, she had none.

The implication is uncomfortable: alignment conceals the problem. If your spontaneous choices happen to match your family’s expectations for long enough, you don’t thereby acquire self-determination — you merely lose the chance to notice you lack it. The real test arrives only when you diverge, and by then you’ve spent twenty years you could have used for practice.

Incidentally, she re-describes her whole academic trajectory the same way: in primary school she was certain she’d be recommended to a particular language-focused middle school, and when the list went up her name wasn’t on it; she wanted a university in Shanghai and didn’t get in; one lunchtime in her final year of high school her deskmate came back from an admissions talk, handed her a brochure, and pointed at a corner of it telling her to register at that URL — she did, and was eventually admitted to HKU off the waitlist. She calls all of it accidental. A track that reads from outside as textbook meritocracy is, in her own retelling, a chain of accidents.

2. Agency was bought, not reasoned into existence Link to heading

This is the heaviest passage in the episode, and the easiest to skim past.

In autumn 2024 she already had the offer — expedition guide in Antarctica, the job she’d dreamed of. She went to tell her father, in her words, “half asking his permission, because that was how I handled things then.” He objected. She turned the job down.

For roughly a month afterward she thought about it every night before sleep. Then the crucial line:

But at the time I didn’t actually know what self-determination was.

She drove to Shanghai to see friends, who told her the word for it, and told her she shouldn’t be deferring everything to her family. She says it was hard to hear at first. When she got back, she worked up the nerve to email and ask whether the position was still open.

It had been filled.

That opportunity was gone for good. She kept petitioning the company and only got out the following winter.

What’s worth recording here is the epistemology. The concept of agency was named for her by friends, not arrived at through introspection — and what actually taught her wasn’t the argument but the lost job. As she puts it later: it was only “when it hurt on my own body” that she understood.

That’s an unromantic answer to “how do you acquire agency”: most likely not by reading the right sentence, but by losing something you could have had. The price is real, and non-refundable.

3. The criterion isn’t how much it hurts — it’s whether you have a next stop Link to heading

At the end, the host asks a good question: “leaving” sounds so forced, so involuntary — will it always be an ongoing process? Will there be a day when you say not “I left,” but “today I’m going to such-and-such place”?

By convention the answer would be “once the wound heals and things soften.”

What Lumi says instead: her second conflict with her family was more severe than the first, and in that moment she was unusually calm. When she left the next day, she no longer experienced it as leaving. Her reason is blunt:

Because I already had a next destination.

That’s much harder than the usual “from fleeing to pursuing” framing — because in her case the quantity of pain went up while the sense of fleeing disappeared. The only thing that changed was whether she had a concrete next stop that didn’t depend on them — and it was a job, one she describes as adding a brick to her own livelihood.

So the operative variable is neither the severity of the harm nor whether the feelings have been processed. This is also what keeps her account from collapsing into self-consolation: she doesn’t deny she was hurt. She simply stops letting it determine which direction she moves.

She’d stated the same logic earlier:

It’s not that I have to resolve everything before I can set out.

Paired with how she handles the cloud over her head — not dispelling it, but carrying it: “I can keep walking my road with these things; maybe I just walk less lightly” — a consistent stance emerges. She treats “unresolved” as the normal condition of departure, not an obstacle.

There’s one more line here I find remarkably honest and easy to skip. She says that for a different person, or a different family, these things “might become fuel for going even further — but I happen not to have that.”

She refuses to convert harm into inspirational fuel. Suffering, for her, is not assigned meaning; it’s just weight. Her method is to carry it, not to claim it made her stronger.

4. “The good place” is a premise she falsified herself Link to heading

She named her own podcast The Good Place. The motivation was a search: after a dozen-plus moves in six years in Hong Kong, she sat one evening in a noisy little rented room wondering when she’d find a good place of her own.

Then she actually went searching — South America, North America, a long planned loop. The result: the more she traveled, the more lost she got. Difficult, exhausting, continuously expensive.

Her conclusion is structurally a falsification rather than a failure. The good place can’t be found not because she didn’t go far enough, but because the requirements are mutually exclusive — you cannot simultaneously have safe, developed, mountains, ocean, city, and cheap. A perfect good place cannot exist by construction. So “good place” gets redefined from a geographic coordinate into a capacity: learning to appreciate what’s already in front of you.

Put it beside section 3 and it completes: the next stop has to be concrete; the good place has to give up on perfect. The first requires you to have a destination; the second requires you not to expect that destination to solve everything. Her travel is sustainable precisely because she lowered her expectations of each individual destination.

Her passage on money, by the way, is the most anti-inspirational part of the episode. The “life is an open field, not a track” genre gets a hard qualifier from her: leaving is emphatically not a matter of working up reckless courage — there has to be accumulation first, and that process “may not be all that comfortable.” She keeps an invisible line, the savings in her account the day she quit; it dipped after she went home, and the goal is to hold it and bring it back up. That’s a quantifiable, monitorable figure, not a feeling.

5. What she offers isn’t a method — it’s an existence proof Link to heading

Later the host asks whether she considers all of this a privilege. She acknowledges it directly, then describes a state that rarely gets discussed: having recognized her own privilege, she felt she shouldn’t speak anymore, and this troubled her for a long stretch.

The chain is logically coherent — my experience isn’t representative, saying it aloud might mislead. But its consequence is that exactly the people with the slack to record and articulate go quiet, and the people who need to see a sample end up with one fewer.

Her resolution converts the question from entitlement to use: given that I have these things, can I avoid wasting them? Entitlement questions are unanswerable and loop forever; use questions can be answered.

And what’s notable about the use she names is that it supplies no method. What she hopes is that some girl, at some moment, remembers that a person like this exists, that she picked up a bag and left — and therefore, so could I.

That distinction is substantive. It’s how she was moved herself: she says the courage to pack that bag came from things she’d read beforehand, other people’s accounts, “maybe just one sentence of theirs that stayed in my head.”

When someone is stuck, what’s usually missing isn’t knowledge of how to proceed. It’s the belief that anyone has walked this road. An existence proof has a far lower bar than a methodology, and may do more work — because it doesn’t require your circumstances to match.

I originally listened to this episode as material (I used it for a controlled experiment on AI writing in another post). But what actually stayed with me afterward are the judgments above. What they share is a refusal of the easier version: not a history of oppression but a coincidence; not figuring it out but paying for it; not healing but carrying; not finding the good place but discovering the question was malformed.
