From Scientific Revolutions to Scientific Evaluation

26.10.17 11-minute read
WM
Philosophy of Science • Kuhn • Research Methods

—Some reflections on Thomas Kuhn’s The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

The Chinese version is also published on the WeChat account Afterthoughts: read the original article.

I. How Kuhn Explains the Development of Science Link to heading

We usually think of science as progressing through the accumulation of knowledge. Scientists discover new facts, propose more accurate theories, and correct past mistakes, gradually deepening our understanding of the world. This account is not wrong, but it may overlook another important kind of change: sometimes scientists change more than their answer to a question. They change their entire way of approaching it.

In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, published in 1962, Thomas S. Kuhn offered an influential account of this kind of change. He argued that science does not always accumulate knowledge along a single path. Its development includes relatively stable periods of research as well as periods in which the underlying research framework undergoes a major transformation.

The following account draws mainly on secondary literature to outline the book’s central ideas, then develops the discussion with reference to Kuhn’s later clarifications.

Paradigms and Normal Science Link to heading

One of Kuhn’s central concepts is the “paradigm.”

In a mature scientific field, researchers usually share an understanding of many basic questions. There is often considerable agreement about which fundamental theories to accept, which problems to study, which methods to use, and what counts as a successful result. Scientists do not have to establish these agreements afresh every time they begin a project. They inherit them through professional education, classic texts, and concrete research practices.

Kuhn used the concept of a paradigm to describe this shared foundation for research. Newtonian mechanics, for example, offered more than a theory of how objects move. It also supplied scientists with further problems to investigate and ways of solving them.

Once a paradigm is established, most research does not repeatedly challenge its basic theories. Instead, it attempts to resolve unfinished problems within the framework. Kuhn called this period “normal science” and compared its principal activity to “puzzle-solving.”

The comparison is revealing. Solving a puzzle presupposes that an answer exists and that the available tools are, in principle, sufficient to find it. Scientists may need to improve their measurements, revise their calculations, or design more precise experiments. But encountering a difficulty does not usually lead them to question the entire theoretical system at once.

Normal science is therefore conservative in some respects, but this does not mean that it lacks creativity. On the contrary, because researchers do not have to keep debating the most basic questions, they can concentrate on pursuing specific problems in greater depth. Much scientific knowledge accumulates in this way.

Anomalies, Crises, and Scientific Revolutions Link to heading

Existing theories cannot, of course, explain every observation.

During their research, scientists may encounter phenomena that do not agree with theoretical predictions. Kuhn called these “anomalies.” Yet the appearance of an anomaly does not immediately overturn a paradigm. Every theory has unresolved problems, and scientists usually try first to find an explanation within the existing framework.

A crisis may develop only when important problems remain unresolved for a long time and researchers begin to doubt whether their existing theories can really address them. This creates an opening for new theories and research methods.

When a new theoretical system gradually replaces the old paradigm, and the scientific community begins conducting research with different concepts, methods, and questions, Kuhn calls the change a “scientific revolution.”

The replacement of the Ptolemaic system by Copernican heliocentrism, the establishment of Newtonian mechanics, and the changes that relativity brought to classical physics are among the important historical cases Kuhn discusses. These developments did more than correct a few mistaken conclusions. They changed the basic framework through which researchers understood natural phenomena.

Kuhn also introduced the concept of “incommensurability.” Scientists working within different paradigms may understand the same concept differently, focus on different questions, and even disagree about what counts as a successful explanation. Choosing between an old theory and a new one therefore cannot always be settled by a completely neutral set of rules.

Incommensurability does not, however, mean that communication between paradigms is impossible, or that scientists can choose theories only according to personal preference. They can still compare how well theories agree with observations, which problems they can solve, and what further research they might make possible. The difficulty is that these considerations may not form a set of rules that leads everyone to the same conclusion.

After a scientific revolution, the new paradigm gradually gains acceptance, and scientists resume normal research within the new framework. On this account, scientific development includes both the accumulation of knowledge within existing theories and changes to the basic framework of research.

This is one of the most important insights of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions: scientific progress does not consist only in adding to what was already known. Sometimes a new theory changes how we understand earlier knowledge, and even which questions are worth asking.

II. Three Reflections on This Account Link to heading

1. When a Classic Theory Becomes Part of Ordinary Research Thinking Link to heading

After becoming acquainted with Kuhn’s main arguments, my first reaction is that many of these ideas no longer seem unfamiliar. This does not diminish their historical importance. But it does raise a question: why can a book that once transformed people’s understanding of science now seem almost self-evident?

Viewed from economics and other applied, empirical disciplines, many of the phenomena Kuhn describes are already part of everyday research.

Researchers typically begin with existing literature and theories, formulate hypotheses, build models, and test them using data or experiments. Different fields also have their own familiar questions, methods, and habits of evaluation. Most research does not attempt to transform an entire discipline at its foundations. It improves models within an existing framework, extends the range of inquiry, or explains phenomena that were not previously well understood.

Researchers also know that every model has assumptions and conditions under which it applies. When observations do not agree with a model, they have to consider whether the problem lies with the data, the method, the assumptions, or the more fundamental theoretical framework.

These practices have much in common with Kuhn’s descriptions of paradigms, normal science, and anomalies. Familiarity with modern empirical research does not, of course, imply acceptance of all his claims about scientific revolutions and incommensurability. Still, a substantial part of his account of how scientists conduct research is now quite easy to recognize.

This may illustrate something interesting about the influence of classic theories. Their influence is not always visible in how often people cite them. Once ideas enter disciplinary education and research habits, they may cease to feel particularly novel. Later researchers may already be using similar ways of thinking without having read the works in which those ideas were developed.

2. Disputes About History and the Explanatory Power of a Theory Link to heading

Kuhn’s account has, of course, attracted considerable criticism. Some disagreements concern whether his description makes the history of science look too orderly.

Does every discipline have a clearly dominant paradigm? Must a crisis precede a scientific revolution? Are changes between old and new theories really as dramatic as the idea of a paradigm shift suggests?

The actual history of science may not divide so neatly into stages. Some theories change gradually through prolonged revision and refinement. Competing research traditions may coexist for a long time. Even when a major theoretical change occurs, the new theory often preserves parts of the old one that remain useful.

These observations remind us that we should not treat Kuhn’s account as a fixed law that every discipline must follow.

On the other hand, a historical explanation does not have to cover every case to be valuable. Theories necessarily simplify complex realities to some extent. What matters is whether that simplification helps us understand phenomena that would otherwise be difficult to see clearly.

Kuhn’s contribution lies precisely in drawing our attention to the shared theories and research habits on which science depends. He also helps us see that some major changes in the history of science involve more than new knowledge replacing old knowledge: the questions researchers ask and the ways they evaluate answers may change together.

When evaluating his account, then, we should ask more than whether it explains every historical development. We should also ask where it has explanatory power, where it needs revision, and whether it overlooks important phenomena.

This does not mean that a theory can explain away counterexamples however it likes. If it can accommodate any historical fact simply by continually redefining its concepts, it loses the ability to distinguish between different situations.

But disagreements about Kuhn are not confined to historical details. There is a more fundamental question: if standards of evaluation can differ between paradigms, on what grounds can scientists regard a new theory as better than an old one? If there is no set of rules everyone accepts, can scientific progress still be explained rationally?

Kuhn addressed this question in greater depth in his later work.

3. Shared Values of Scientific Evaluation Beneath Changing Paradigms Link to heading

After publishing The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, Kuhn continued to clarify and develop his views.

In the postscript written in 1969 and included in the second edition in 1970, he further explained what he meant by a paradigm and introduced the concept of a “disciplinary matrix.” Alongside accepted theories, models, and exemplars, a scientific community also shares evaluative values concerning what makes a good theory or good research.

In his 1977 essay “Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice,” Kuhn discussed more specifically five criteria that scientists commonly use when choosing theories.

Accuracy: a theory’s predictions and consequences should agree as closely as possible with observation and experiment.

Consistency: a theory should not contradict itself, and should be as compatible as possible with widely accepted knowledge in related fields.

Scope: a good theory should explain more than the few phenomena it was initially designed to address. It should apply to a wider range of problems.

Simplicity: a theory should explain phenomena as clearly and simply as possible, particularly by bringing together phenomena that previously appeared unrelated.

Fruitfulness: a theory should do more than explain established facts. It should also generate new research questions and help scientists discover previously unknown phenomena or relationships.

Kuhn did not invent these criteria, nor did he suggest that scientists can consider only these five aspects when evaluating theories. His point was precisely that, although these are widely recognized grounds for evaluation, they do not automatically tell us which theory to choose.

One theory, for example, may explain existing data more accurately but require highly complicated assumptions. Another may be simpler and offer greater promise for explaining new phenomena, while remaining unable to address every known problem.

Scientists can accept the same criteria yet disagree about their relative importance. They may also understand a particular criterion differently. What counts as sufficiently simple? How much prediction error is acceptable? How much weight should we give a new theory that has not yet been thoroughly tested but appears promising for further research? None of these questions can be answered simply by calculating a score.

Kuhn therefore understood these criteria as shared evaluative values, rather than an algorithm that automatically determines which theory is superior.

This also explains how scientists can disagree about a theoretical question while sharing research norms. Their disagreement need not mean that someone is irrational or disregarding evidence. They may be weighing the strengths and weaknesses of theories differently, making different judgments about the evidence and the criteria used to assess it.

What matters here is more than the particular five criteria Kuhn listed. It is a more general possibility: even as scientific theories, research methods, and specific paradigms change, scientists may continue to share relatively stable evaluative values.

These values are not immutable. Scientists in different historical periods may understand accuracy, simplicity, or a theory’s research potential differently, and may assign them different importance. But those values do not disappear entirely with each theoretical change.

Scientists can therefore discuss the strengths and weaknesses of theories meaningfully without possessing a single algorithm for evaluating them. Even if they ultimately disagree, they can at least explain why they consider one theory more worthy of acceptance.

Our understanding of Kuhn can thus extend beyond the historical development of science to the question of how scientists evaluate research.

This question also deserves consideration in investment research. Some research can draw on large amounts of historical data, statistical tests, and out-of-sample validation to support investment judgments. Other research concerns emerging technologies, specialized industries, or distinctive companies for which sufficiently comparable cases are hard to find. Yet judgments must still be made on the basis of specific facts, expectations about the future, and current market prices. These approaches may rely on different kinds of evidence and validation, but both face the question of how to assess the reliability of their conclusions.

The evaluative values of scientific research can inform this discussion, though they cannot be equated directly with criteria for investment decisions. For investing, the further questions are: what evidence is sufficient to support an investment judgment? And under what conditions does that judgment warrant an actual position?
