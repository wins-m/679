Understanding a Philosophy Does Not Mean Accepting Its Answers

26.10.19 8-minute read
WM
Philosophy • Schopenhauer • Reading

—Reflections on reading Schopenhauer

I first wanted to learn about Schopenhauer’s The World as Will and Representation because of questions about desire, suffering, personal autonomy, and social relationships. I hoped that reading philosophy would offer a perspective from which to understand them.

But I did not read the original work from beginning to end. Instead, I tried to grasp its main claims through introductory readings and conversation, then examine their arguments and limitations. What I had imagined as a systematic reading of a philosophical classic gradually became something else. I found that I was less concerned with reconstructing the entire philosophical system than with understanding what these ideas could actually tell me.

I. A Philosophy That Tries to Explain the Whole World Link to heading

Schopenhauer’s philosophy has an impressive unity. He holds that the world we know is only the world as representation, while its inner essence is a blind, ceaseless will. Human desires and actions are expressions of this will, as are the unconscious forces and movements of nature.

From this, he develops an account of the human condition. The will continually gives rise to desires. When they go unsatisfied, we suffer; once they are satisfied, we may fall into boredom or develop new desires. It is therefore difficult to attain lasting happiness by continually satisfying our desires. His proposed response is equally radical: art can temporarily free us from the will’s control, compassion allows us to move beyond the limits of individual interest, and complete liberation points toward the denial of the will to life.

This system can bring nature, desire, suffering, art, and ethics within a single explanation. Yet I gradually came to realize that the unity of an explanation does not mean that it reveals a real, shared essence.

II. Does Explaining Phenomena Mean Understanding Their Essence? Link to heading

Schopenhauer holds that gravity, plant growth, and other physical processes in nature can all be understood as objectifications of the will. My initial question was: can these phenomena not be explained through physical laws, thermodynamic processes, and biological evolution?

Of course, increasing entropy alone cannot explain every natural phenomenon, and processes at different levels have their own mechanisms. But this made me wonder further: if these phenomena have their own natural explanations, why must we also suppose that the same will lies behind them all?

Schopenhauer’s will is not a mysterious force that exists in addition to the forces of nature. He is trying to answer a metaphysical question beyond scientific explanation: what is the inner essence of natural forces? Yet there seems to be an inferential gap that is difficult to bridge between our direct experience of our own will and the claim that all of nature shares the same inner essence. If anything we observe can be explained as an expression of the will, what does this explanation rule out? And how much understanding does it offer independently of the existing descriptions of those phenomena?

This reminds me of explanations of mechanisms in investment research. We may find variables that predict asset returns and then propose an economic narrative for the statistical relationship. Even if the predictive relationship is supported out of sample, that does not establish that the mechanism described by the narrative is the true cause. The same observations may be compatible with several explanations.

Of course, metaphysics and empirical science have different aims and cannot simply be judged by identical standards. Still, both remind me that constructing an explanation from phenomena and establishing that the explanation reveals the actual mechanism are two different things. The fact that science has yet to answer an ultimate question does not, by itself, lend support to any particular unified metaphysical explanation.

III. From Desire to Suffering, and Then to the Denial of the Will Link to heading

Even if we set aside Schopenhauer’s account of the universe, his observations about desire remain meaningful. Reason does not always govern our behavior. Once one desire is satisfied, another may arise. Some goals seem immensely important before we achieve them, yet fail to bring the lasting satisfaction we expected. These experiences are familiar, but I no longer think they lead directly to the conclusion that life is essentially suffering.

Continual striving does not necessarily mean continual suffering. A person can keep exploring new questions, developing abilities, or taking part in an activity without regarding the absence of final satisfaction as a defect. The pursuit itself may be a part of life worth affirming. Likewise, even if we accept that suffering is inevitable, it does not follow directly that the highest ideal in life is the denial of the will to life.

For Schopenhauer, denying the will means more than reducing a few desires. It is a fundamental rejection of a way of existing centered on continual striving. This is an extreme spiritual and ethical ideal, rather than an ordinary adjustment to how one lives. But why must ceasing to desire be better than having something to pursue? Why should reducing suffering be the highest standard by which we evaluate life? Explaining the world as will does not automatically answer these questions.

I gradually came to see that Schopenhauer’s philosophy involves at least several distinct steps: first observing human desire and suffering, then explaining them through a unified will, then judging this condition of existence negatively, and finally proposing liberation through the denial of the will. Each step needs its own argument. Accepting the initial observations does not require us to accept the subsequent explanations and value judgments.

IV. Understanding a Predicament Does Not Mean Finding Its Solution Link to heading

During this reading, I had hoped that discussing Schopenhauer’s claims would gradually help me determine whether they were right or wrong. Later, however, I found that some questions may never admit of a clear and conclusive proof. Of course, this does not mean that all philosophical claims are equally reasonable, or that the soundness of an argument no longer matters. It simply means that, for me, spending much more time investigating the ultimate truth or falsity of a metaphysical claim may not yield a corresponding gain in understanding.

More significantly, even accepting an explanation of the world or human nature does not mean that it can directly answer practical questions about life. Why someone desires a particular career, income, social recognition, or intimate relationship, for example, is a question about the origins of desire. Whether that desire is worth pursuing is another question.

A wish may be shaped by the social environment and still have real value. A wish may arise entirely from one’s own inclinations and still carry an unbearable cost. Knowing where a desire comes from is not enough to decide how to respond to it. Likewise, recognizing that a certain kind of suffering may recur does not mean that we must fundamentally reject the capacity to desire.

Practical judgments still require us to consider specific circumstances, constraints, available alternatives, and the costs we are willing to bear. Here, Schopenhauer can help me identify problems, but he cannot make my choices for me.

V. Moving Beyond a Single Philosophical Perspective Link to heading

At this point in my reading, a new question began to occur to me: why should Schopenhauer be my main framework for understanding these issues?

He begins with desire and suffering and treats the denial of the will as ultimate liberation. But the history of philosophy clearly offers many other answers. Aristotle understands the good life through human activity, capacities, and virtues. Nietzsche questions why suffering and conflict must count against life. Pragmatism pays more attention to how people can improve their lives through action and reflection in particular circumstances. Theories of social recognition and freedom place individual circumstances within interpersonal relationships and institutional settings.

These ideas are not necessarily more correct than Schopenhauer’s, nor can they simply be assembled into a complete answer free of contradictions. But they at least remind me that the same question can have different explanatory starting points, and that different explanations may lead to very different value judgments. If we know only one philosopher well, we can easily come to accept, without noticing, the boundaries he sets around the questions and the evaluative standards he assumes.

What I now hope to understand, then, is how several different philosophical traditions relate to one another, rather than continuing deeper into a single system until I can defend or refute every one of its claims.

Looking back, this reading did not give me a philosophy that could directly guide my life. But it made me more clearly aware that explaining the world, evaluating life, and deciding how to act are three related questions, none of which can simply stand in for the others.

A philosophy may raise important questions without offering convincing explanations. It may offer valuable explanations without determining what kind of life we should pursue. Perhaps, for me, the point of reading philosophy is not ultimately to accept one philosopher’s answers, but to gradually recognize how many other ways there are to understand the world I face.
