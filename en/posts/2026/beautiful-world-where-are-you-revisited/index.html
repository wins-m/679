Beautiful World, Where Are You? (Revisited)

26.9.20 9-minute read
WM
Film • 5 Centimeters per Second • Memory • Life

30-second summary: The post I wrote yesterday read more like film analysis of the live-action 5 Centimeters per Second. Thinking it over again today, I realized what actually stayed with me wasn’t the narrative itself, but an unplaceable sense of familiarity — and the question it drags along with it: when, and how quietly, did I stop believing that things would naturally get better? This post doesn’t answer that question. It just looks at it more clearly.

The first pass, closer to film analysis, is here.

Watching the live-action 5 Centimeters per Second brought back Sally Rooney’s Beautiful World, Where Are You. It’s strange — I barely remember what that book was about anymore.

I remember its cover, its title, even the fact that I read it. But when I try to recall its characters and story, almost nothing comes back. The animated 5 Centimeters per Second is the same — I watched it once, and have forgotten most of its plot.

But watching the live-action film, I kept feeling a strange sense of familiarity. Not because I remembered some specific person, or some specific event — more often it was just light, faces, streets, windows, sky, and fragments from early growing-up years with no particular meaning, suddenly making me feel I had once been there.

I started to suspect that memory doesn’t always survive in the shape of a story. Sometimes what survives is only light. Or a certain color. Or the feeling of someone looking out a window one afternoon. The film, then, wasn’t replaying my memories — it was offering an entrance through which things that had no shape to begin with could reappear.

I don’t know how long this feeling will last. Maybe in a few months I’ll have forgotten most of this film’s plot too. But I might remember that, on some afternoon, watching those images on the screen, I suddenly recalled something I can no longer name precisely.

Where did that beautiful world go? Link to heading

The film’s first half was unforgettable. It was a beautiful world — not a grand or fantastical one, but small things: children’s faces, school, winter light, streets, train stations, and a kind of relationship between people that reality hadn’t yet fully worn down. It was almost too beautiful.

So I started to wonder:

Where did such a world go, afterward?

Did it really exist? Or does memory simply lay a soft light over the past once a person is old enough to look back? If that world is only a beautification of memory, then 5 Centimeters per Second might just be a story about attachment and nostalgia.

But there’s another question. If such a world really did exist, why did some people live inside it while others did not? To me, that question is harder to answer than “why do people lose their first love as they grow up” — because what I felt in the film wasn’t only empathy for a character. There was also a more personal, harder-to-explain regret:

Growing up could once have been like that.

Mine may not have been.

What was never had can still be lost Link to heading

I used to think “loss” had to mean having once possessed something. Later I realized that isn’t quite right — some things may never have truly belonged to me, and I can still feel regret over their absence.

The children in the film hold something I can’t fully confirm I ever had: a belief in the future. A belief that simply by growing up, the world would naturally widen. A belief that beyond this present life, there would still be an “afterward.” A belief that outside school, outside the city, outside youth, a richer world was waiting.

This belief didn’t need to be spoken. It didn’t even need a concrete goal. Taking forever on homework as a kid; spending an afternoon in a café; flipping through books in a library; finishing a film; going for a buffet after an internship ended — none of those things changed anyone’s life, but at the time, they didn’t seem to need to. Life could just be life.

Later, people are expected to answer for the future: study for exams, exams for further schooling, schooling for a job, a job for a better life. There’s nothing obviously wrong with that path. It’s only much further along that I realized the “afterward” I kept deferring never arrived the way I first imagined it would.

It’s not that the world suddenly turned bad. Quite the opposite — life can become more stable, and come with more choices. But sometimes, once a person has walked a long enough road, they suddenly discover:

What I actually lost wasn’t a place — it was the belief that “afterward” would naturally get better.

Beautiful World, Where Are You? Link to heading

So I still can’t quite work out what the Beautiful World in the film actually is. It’s probably not a specific place — not Tokyo, not a hometown, not a particular era. I now even suspect it may never have belonged to the category of things one “has,” because when I think back on the moments that truly made the world feel beautiful to me, they were rarely about gaining something. More often, I had simply stepped into a moment:

A mountain road outside the city, stopping at some bend. Moving through an unfamiliar city, a train passing scenery outside the window. Reading in a quiet room, or walking out of a bookshop having finished half a book. Riding home as a huge city slips from dusk into night. Wandering a foreign university campus, surrounded by young crowds. Walking slowly along a river or the sea, coffee in hand. Watching a film all the way through in a theater, then looking at the credits once the lights come up. Coasting down a long, gentle slope on a bike. Getting a photo that finally matches what I’d imagined. Finishing a small piece of work that satisfies me. Running 4 kilometers, 10 kilometers, or further. Ending a long journey, feet touching unfamiliar ground for the first time. Watching the sun rise over the horizon as a city slowly wakes.

There doesn’t seem to be any shared “meaning” among these moments. Sometimes there isn’t even a purpose. But they share one feeling:

I’d be willing to stay here five more minutes.

Something hasn’t fully ended. The next thing hasn’t yet begun. I’m still moving, not yet arrived — or I’ve already arrived, but I’m in no hurry to leave. The world doesn’t seem to have finished closing in.

Reality isn’t beyond saving Link to heading

That’s why I’ve become less and less willing to simply read this feeling as “reality has gotten worse.” Reality certainly has plenty to be disappointed about. But it isn’t beyond saving. A person can still travel, change cities, meet new people, earn more money, live somewhere better, have more freedom.

The real question is:

If all of that can be had, what do I actually still want?

Sometimes a thing arrives in your hands and the feeling doesn’t come with it. I can go to a more glamorous city. I can go to an unfamiliar country. I can have more choices. But “having” itself doesn’t seem able to give that world back to me.

Maybe what I’m actually looking for was never a Beautiful World that can be possessed — but a small, sudden wish that surfaces sometimes:

I want to go there.

Or:

I want to stay five more minutes.

That wish doesn’t need a reason. It doesn’t even need to prove it has value.

If there’s no “afterward,” what’s left worth looking forward to? Link to heading

For a long time I understood life as a road heading forward. Looking back now, what actually changed may be my understanding of “forward.”

The effort of childhood carried a very simple image behind it:

I’ll keep going, because things will be better afterward.

Later that image slowly became:

I’ll keep going, or things will get worse.

The two sentences look almost the same. But an entirely different world sits between them. The first means the world is still open. The second means only: don’t fall.

So I started to wonder:

If not for “afterward,” where would I still walk toward on my own?

I don’t have an answer right now, and I’m not in a hurry to get one — because the moment I turn it into a goal, I might start living for some “future self” all over again.

Maybe what’s worth doing now is simply paying attention again to very small things: what makes me suddenly want to do something, what leaves me feeling “not bad” once it’s done, what place I was already about to leave and yet stayed five more minutes in. None of it needs to become a plan, and none of it needs to be summed up into some new way of living. Just watch what grows back on its own.

As part of reality Link to heading

Writing this far, I think what moved me most about 5 Centimeters per Second may not be the story it tells, but the realization it gave me:

The film itself is also part of reality.

I used to think the world on screen was fiction, and real life happened outside it. But it doesn’t look that way now — sitting in the theater, watching that light, those characters, those landscapes, I really was there, and those feelings really happened to me. Even without being able to fully map myself onto any character in the film, I could still be touched, in some moment, by that world.

So a Beautiful World doesn’t have to exist outside reality. It doesn’t even have to exist forever. It can just be a beam of light on some afternoon, an aimless walk, a book half-finished, an unfamiliar street, a stretch of downhill riding, those few seconds after a film ends before the lights fully come up.

Maybe what’s called a “beautiful world” was never a place you have to find and move into permanently. It just appears now and then — and we happen to be there.

I don’t know whether I’ll ever believe in that “afterward” again, or whether this feeling will fade once more. But for now, at least, I don’t much want to keep asking:

Where exactly is the Beautiful World?

Maybe what’s really worth remembering are those moments I once, suddenly, was willing to stop and stay five minutes longer. Maybe the world was never waiting there. Maybe it never truly belonged to anyone. And all I can do is, whenever it happens to appear, know:

Yes — I’m willing to be here.
