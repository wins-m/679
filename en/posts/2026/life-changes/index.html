Before the Future Is Certain

26.9.26 14-minute read
WM
Personal Essay • Life • Cats • Film

If I try to look back and describe the past few years, I find it hard to tell a clear story. Some things I no longer remember. Last Lunar New Year, for instance, I barely knew what I was doing. In the year before it I had graduated, started a real job, and begun preparing to leave it, and the hours I was expected to be at my desk kept growing — yet looking back now, it feels as if nothing in that stretch of time really happened.

Maybe the one thing I was clearest about then was that I could accept a fixed life less and less: five days a week in the office, going to bed later and later, sleeping less and less, and a feeling that I had to keep handing myself over just to be allowed to go on living. Looking back, much of what I did afterwards seems to have started there.

I. I used to think leaving work was freedom Link to heading

I made the decision to leave my first real job during a period that wasn’t a happy one. But at the time I genuinely felt free. It didn’t feel like “losing a job”. It felt like I was finally walking, of my own accord, toward a life I had always wanted — trading the time that had already been worn away for time I could spend as I chose.

In the days after I quit, for the first time I got to enjoy, for a long stretch, a small room that was entirely my own, where nobody made me do anything. I also went on a small adventure: in a single day I rode my road bike all the way from my old place to my new home. After that I spent a whole month preparing for a trip to Japan. The preparation itself wore me out a little, but looking back it was still worth it — because in the end it became part of the trip.

I don’t want to record that trip in detail here. It was too dense and too deep; I know I will keep coming back to almost every fragment of it. Partway through I even changed my return flight and stretched the trip out by a few more days. One thing it taught me grew clearer and clearer over time:

What a real trip needs may not be money, but time.

If all you have is public holidays, a so-called trip easily turns into a rapid hop between places to spend money — it might not even be as good as going to the cinema and seriously watching one film. If I have to compare it to something, I would rather compare it to watching Rohmer’s “Tales of the Four Seasons” all the way through. A trip that can actually catch a moment of life needs a lot of time — to wait, to prepare, to enter, and to stay. And a job with no time, or with only public holidays, can hardly offer a life like that.

II. I started trying to find a new kind of life Link to heading

After I came back from the trip, I spent a rather special period at home. I read books for a few months. At the time I felt that what was in those books was what I needed most, and only second came figuring out how to find a remote job so this life could go on. I even seriously worked out my minimum cost of living: counting only food, it was lower than I’d expected — not counting housing, of course, or the electronics I had bought one after another while I was still employed.

Then I finished the books, and what was left on the shelf gradually became things I didn’t see much point in reading, so I started playing games. For a month I played console games almost every day, mostly Wuchang: Fallen Feathers and Elden Ring. The shock Elden Ring gave me is something I still can’t forget. It’s a world out of humanity’s dreams, and I still count it as a kind of luck that I got to live through a game like that in this era. It made me feel, very concretely, for the first time: simply being alive now gives you things that people before us could never have had.

I also did some development practice and published a few small web pages. None of that matters much anymore — a lot of it, today’s AI tools can finish in ten minutes. But back then I had no idea what the future would turn into.

III. What I really wanted all along was to live on the move Link to heading

When I quit, “unemployment” wasn’t what I was after. What I wanted was remote work, and the geographic freedom that comes with it. I used to believe that if I could find a truly remote job, I could leave my old life behind, go somewhere cheaper and freer, and work while I lived.

Later I really did find a remote job, only to discover that living on the move hadn’t come any closer because of it. It made me realise that “quitting” and “remote work” were both only means; the life I had wanted in the first place wasn’t going to arrive on its own just because the form of work had changed.

It also made me look back at my desk-job years. I’ve found that I’m the kind of person who, faced with something I truly believe in, throws myself into it naturally; but when something is reduced to nothing more than an exchange of time, I find it hard to see any meaning in it. More than being arranged, what I need is a reason I’m willing to put myself into.

IV. Mahjong once became my whole world Link to heading

For the better part of a year I hardly watched films anymore, and stopped logging anything on Douban. I rarely went out; the seasons turned a few times, my life didn’t change, and almost all of my free time went to mahjong.

I joined tournament groups and got to know all kinds of people: people who didn’t work, people between jobs, people with busy jobs who squeezed in time to play, people for whom mahjong had become a hobby, people who organised tournaments, students, and some players who, for health reasons, could hardly leave the house. I came to realise that the mahjong circle is itself a very small society, with every kind of person in it. Some of them needed a lot of help beyond mahjong, but seemed to find it very hard to truly accept help from others. So the most stable connection between us was simply playing mahjong together.

During that time I didn’t think of reaching out to anyone either. No particular desire to buy things, no interest in what I ate, no films, no books, no Douban. Most importantly — in all that time, I almost never thought of the people from before. My world became very small. I just kept opening the next game.

Looking back now, I find myself thinking of mahjong alongside the way AI later changed how I work. Mahjong is a game where machines have long been able to calculate better than most people; and at work, too, I gradually went from writing line by line and editing paragraph by paragraph to pushing things forward together with AI — reading its results, judging what’s right and what’s wrong. Both kept bringing me back to the same question: when a machine already does something better than people, where is there left for a person to stand? I don’t know why I put these two things together, but they do seem to belong to the same era. More and more I feel that living through these changes firsthand isn’t necessarily a bad thing.

V. Mahjong ended Link to heading

Later, I stopped playing mahjong altogether. In a sense, it was a full stop I put on that part of my life myself.

It gave me back some time, and I began to face myself again. Of course, facing myself didn’t make things easier right away; some things that mahjong had been covering up slowly surfaced. But no longer playing mahjong was, in itself, still a positive change for me.

VI. A journey made clearer what I don’t want Link to heading

Before all this, I once went on a long road trip. Looking back, it didn’t leave me with anything I especially wanted to continue. Neither the route nor the way it began was my decision; what I went through along the way was a feeling of things being out of control, of not being my own master, of simply being carried along. During that time I often thought of certain people from my past and drew a little comfort from the memories; I’d also talk with friends about how ill at ease that journey made me.

Later I stayed on my own for a while in a city along the way, and that turned out to be the part of the trip that made me truly happy. I went about alone — went wherever I wanted, wandered out whenever I felt like it, ate whatever I wanted. It suddenly became clear to me:

Living alone doesn’t mean being lonely.

Much of the time, what actually made me uncomfortable was having my life arranged by someone else’s pace and someone else’s reasons. The trip also made me realise that buying a car would be a very barren kind of consumption for me. It just carries a person from one place of spending to another, and along the way you may become more and more wrapped up in society, and lose more and more of your sense of nature and the real world.

After that trip I went back to work. It also got me thinking more seriously about how to weigh what a job adds to a life against what it takes out of it.

VII. I began to doubt my old picture of the world Link to heading

For a long time I carried an idea of “elsewhere” — as if there were some place in the world that was simply better, and once I got there, everything would be different. Later, some concrete experiences broke that picture. What I saw was a world order that made me uncomfortable: in front of certain systems, people get sorted by identity, money and eligibility.

But I don’t want to simply reject that other world because of it; I’d rather stay open now. It’s just that I’m less and less willing to believe any place naturally belongs to a “better” world — snobbery, control, and equating people with fame and money can exist anywhere. What’s really worth looking for may never have been a country, but a life in which I can exist as a person.

VIII. And now, I’ve met a cat Link to heading

Not long ago, for a while I kept feeling stuck in some kind of loop. I seriously considered just buying a plane ticket and going somewhere farther away for a stretch — go first, find the most basic place to stay, then find a café with internet. I also tried staying somewhere else for a few days, but in the end I went home, and life slipped back into its old rhythm. I began to suspect that what I lacked wasn’t a perfect plan at all, but that I needed to actually break out of this loop.

On one of those nights, out on a walk, I met a little stray cat. It was very friendly. I had no food with me, but it followed me all the way, right to the foot of my building. So I took it home — dewormed it, set up a place for it to live, and watched it grow from about 700 grams to a little over a kilo now.

That coincidence changed me. Suddenly I had a reason I couldn’t argue with to step away from the computer. Spending time with it every day, I slip into a very simple, focused state. I started to find:

What a day means isn’t decided only by how much I got done.

Something even happened that my past self would have found hard to believe — I no longer feel a day has to produce something in order not to be wasted. I can simply be with a cat, and that in itself can be a stretch of life.

IX. 5 Centimeters per Second brought the past back Link to heading

In the middle of these changes, I recently watched 5 Centimeters per Second. Its effect on me may be one of the most important upgrades in my whole history of watching films — one of the most important moments of my life, even. What first truly struck me was the children’s faces in the live-action film, and I wrote two pieces about it. Then I watched the anime and read the novel, and everything became completely different. I thought about it for a whole week straight, and I still haven’t finished digesting it.

At first the film made me think of a closed world I had imagined for myself, one that no longer held any possibility. But after reading the novel, for several nights afterwards I couldn’t put it down. This time, what I thought of was no longer fragments of childhood, but someone who was once very important in my life. What really struck me in the novel was probably that line: “I believe you’ll be fine, Takaki-kun. I’m sure of it.”

I suddenly realised that what matters most isn’t whether that person is still in my life now, or what each of us later became, but that such a person really did once appear in my life. And so that person from the past is enough to keep telling me:

You’ll be fine.

I shouldn’t keep being so hard on myself and so betray the kindness that person gave me back then. No matter how long it has been, I shouldn’t betray the wish that once existed there.

That sentence suddenly connected with so much of what I had done over the past few years. I had always been searching for a certain future: I wanted to find a remote job, a place where I could live on the move, a life that nobody else could control; I wanted to prove I could keep living by some means or other. But the future never gave me any certainty, and I even kept pushing myself harder and harder because of that uncertainty, thinking that only if I forced myself into some position would the future not cave in. But now, for the first time, I feel that perhaps what I should really do is the opposite:

I need to make choices genuinely out of respect and care for myself, not for some abstract future that doesn’t yet exist.

X. Maybe this is what really changed these past few years Link to heading

If I put all of this together, it seems I have been trying, over and over, to leave something: leave the office, leave mahjong, leave a journey that wasn’t mine to steer, leave the mechanical movement between places of spending, leave a way of living where I must constantly prove my worth — and even leave the self who believes “if I don’t keep pushing, the future will surely go bad”.

But only now do I realise that what I really want to leave may not be any of these particular things, but a way of living:

Constantly sacrificing the self who is really here, right now, for an abstract future.

I used to think freedom meant having enough money, a remote job, and a place where I could live on the move for a long time. Later I found that freedom can also just mean being able to go out for a walk when I feel like it, being able to decide for myself whether to leave, being able to wander alone through an unfamiliar city, being able to change the day’s plans because of a stray cat, being able to sit down and seriously watch a film, being able to decide to go easier on myself because someone from the past once told me “you’ll be fine”. These things all look small, but perhaps they are life itself.

I don’t know what next year will bring. I don’t even know whether I’ll end up living somewhere farther away, whether I’ll go study film, whether I’ll travel for a long time again. And I don’t seem to be in much of a hurry to know, because at least I already know one thing:

The future doesn’t have to prove itself worthy before I’m allowed to live today in earnest.

And the things that really happened — a trip, a film, a cat, a sentence from one person, a decision to go out for a walk one night — don’t become meaningless just because the future didn’t turn out as I pictured it. Perhaps what I’ve really gained these past few years isn’t a road to some certain future, but finally beginning to believe:

I can keep going.

Because someone once believed that about me.
