From Scientific Evaluation to Investment Choices

26.10.17 7-minute read
WM
Philosophy of Science • Investment Research • Research Methods

—Some reflections on research paradigms and evaluative values

A model may explain past markets very well without making money in the future. Conversely, a highly successful investment does not necessarily imply that a reliable investment method lies behind it. What, then, makes investment research good research?

In the previous essay on Thomas Kuhn’s The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, I discussed scientific paradigms and the shared evaluative values behind them. Kuhn argued that scientists working with different theories may still share standards for judging what makes a good theory, although those standards cannot form an algorithm that automatically determines which theory is superior.

This led me to wonder what might follow if we brought this way of thinking into investment research.

I. Does Investment Research Have Paradigms Too? Link to heading

For Kuhn, a paradigm is more than a particular theory or research method. It also includes the problems, tools, theoretical commitments, and exemplars shared by a research community. There seem to be comparable research traditions in investing.

Market making focuses on liquidity, order flow, and inventory risk. Arbitrage research looks for pricing discrepancies between related assets. Equity research at medium and low frequencies may use historical data to identify relationships between company characteristics and future returns. Fundamental investing may begin with a particular company’s operations, competitive advantages, and growth prospects. Each practice has familiar problems, tools, and accumulated experience. Yet it is difficult to place them under a single dominant paradigm of the kind Kuhn describes in normal science. They can coexist for long periods and can be combined. Investment research also seems to lack the clear process of paradigm revolution found in his account of scientific history.

A more fundamental difference is that investment activity does not aim to produce scientific knowledge. Investors may base decisions on systematic empirical research, an understanding of individual companies, or even a fleeting market opportunity. The analogy between investment research and scientific paradigms is therefore limited. Still, if different research traditions do share some evaluative standards, Kuhn’s discussion of scientific values may offer useful insights.

II. Are the Values of Scientific Research Also the Values of Investment Research? Link to heading

In “Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice,” Kuhn discussed five values involved in choosing scientific theories: accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness. These remain meaningful in investment research, but their relative importance may differ.

Scientific theories, for example, are often valued for explaining a wider range of phenomena. Investment research need not work this way. A judgment applicable only to one unusual company may have considerable economic value. It does not have to explain the entire market; it only has to offer useful guidance when a particular opportunity arises. The absence of comparable cases, however, also makes that judgment harder to validate.

Simplicity also deserves reconsideration. Kuhn’s conception concerns how a theory organizes and explains phenomena simply and clearly. In quantitative research, researchers also often prefer models with simple structures, hoping to reduce the risk of overfitting. But a factor expressed by a simple formula may have emerged from thousands of attempts. Multiple testing and selection bias arising from extensive experimentation are problems of statistical reliability; a simple final expression does not rule them out.

Accuracy raises another question. A multifactor model may have an elegant mathematical structure and explain historical differences in returns well, without possessing sufficient predictive power for the future. Even if a predictive relationship remains stable, the economic returns ultimately available are another matter. A model may be valuable for understanding markets or managing risk, but these uses differ from directly generating investment returns.

These examples suggest that even where scientific research and investment research share evaluative values, the achievements being evaluated are not quite the same. A theory’s success in explaining market phenomena does not necessarily translate directly into investment success.

III. When Research Meets Real Markets Link to heading

When research enters actual investing, a more immediate question arises: under what conditions can these insights support investment decisions? Research evidence is only part of the answer; market prices, returns and risks, and practical conditions of execution also matter.

A statistically significant signal may be difficult to use because its potential returns are too small or its trading costs too high. A judgment about an unusual company, despite lacking sufficient comparable historical cases, may still identify an investment opportunity worth considering. Different pools of capital also have different return objectives, risk tolerances, and liquidity constraints, which affect how investors evaluate research. The same method may therefore have very different appeal to different investors.

Repeatability is another value worth considering. One successful investment can hardly establish that the research method itself is effective. Markets keep changing. Researchers may never encounter the same opportunity again, and past success alone cannot distinguish skill from luck. Even when a method has repeatedly generated returns historically, we still have to consider whether the market conditions on which it depends will persist.

There is another question here that puzzles me: how much independent value does explanation have? In scientific research, explaining a phenomenon may itself be an important achievement. In investment practice, an explanation often also has to show whether it can help us predict the future or judge when a return pattern might break down.

Should we give a lower evaluation to a strategy that continues to make money but lacks a clear economic explanation? Conversely, how should we regard a logically persuasive theory that cannot generate substantial returns? Investment research seems unable to evaluate a method solely by its explanatory power or solely by its realized returns.

Returning to Kuhn’s discussion of shared values, we may accept similar evaluative standards yet assign them different weights because our research objectives, understanding of markets, and practical circumstances differ. But a method’s suitability for an investment objective does not establish that it can achieve that objective. The reasons for choosing a method and the evidence for its effectiveness remain two separate questions.

IV. From Research Methods Back to Research Objectives Link to heading

Thinking about these questions has also led me to reconsider some of my habitual assumptions about investment research.

Familiarity with statistical models makes it natural to look for patterns that can be validated through large numbers of historical observations. Experience with factor research draws attention to statistical significance, out-of-sample stability, and scope of application. There is nothing inherently wrong with these standards. But as they become the default measures of research quality, I may stop asking so often what problem the research was meant to solve in the first place.

One method, for example, may identify weak but stable predictive relationships across a broad universe of stocks. Another may seek to identify a very small number of companies with exceptional growth opportunities. Their research objects, available evidence, and potential return structures differ, and they may not be suited to exactly the same evaluative standards. The former can be tested using historical samples across stocks and time. The latter may require assessing the credibility of a judgment against specific facts about a company’s operations, rather than relaxing the requirement for reliable evidence.

In the past, I was more accustomed to starting with familiar methods and looking for worthwhile research questions. Now I am beginning to think that I should consider more carefully what I want research to accomplish, and which methods are worth learning, adopting, and developing. These views may also continue to change through the research process.

This has led me to reconsider the place of statistical validation, theoretical explanation, and repeatability in investment research. I still value them; I am simply less certain that they should be the default standards for evaluating all investment research.

I have not yet developed a sufficiently clear set of standards for evaluating investment research. But Kuhn’s discussion of shared values has helped me recognize that, even before choosing a research method, we are already making judgments about what kinds of research achievements are worth pursuing. Those evaluative standards, which I had not previously examined closely, may be precisely what deserves to be thought through first.
