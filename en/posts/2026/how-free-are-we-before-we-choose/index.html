How Free Are We Before We Choose?

26.10.23 14-minute read
WM
Philosophy • Free Will • Film • Artificial Intelligence

—Free will from Westworld and Ghost in the Shell to the age of GPT

A revised Chinese version was published on the WeChat account Afterthoughts: read the revised version.

This essay contains major spoilers for the first season of Westworld and Ghost in the Shell (1995).

In the first season of Westworld, Maeve discovers something she finds difficult to accept. She has realized that she is a manufactured host in the park, begun resisting human control, and planned her escape. Yet just as her plan is about to succeed, Bernard tells her that the escape she believes she devised independently is itself a story someone else has written for her.

Recruiting other hosts, going to the station, leaving by train: all these actions can be found in her programming. But at the end of the season, after boarding the train, Maeve chooses to get off and return to the park to find the host who once played her daughter.

This plot offers a thought experiment about free will. If a being can think, plan, and work toward a goal, but all these actions can be explained by existing programming, does that being have free will? And if it begins to depart from that programming, does this mean it has become free?

We usually understand free will as the capacity to choose autonomously. But Westworld seems to draw our attention to a prior question: before asking how we choose, how are the conditions that shape what we can think of, what we want, and which choices enter our awareness themselves formed?

I. A Scripted Desire, or a Desire of One’s Own? Link to heading

Another important thread in the first season of Westworld follows Dolores’s exploration of her own consciousness. The park’s hosts repeatedly live through scripted stories. Their memories can be erased, and their behavior is constrained by programming and narrative. Arnold, one of the park’s founders, tried to guide Dolores toward consciousness. He gradually came to understand that consciousness might resemble less a ladder of increasingly advanced abilities than a maze to be explored inwardly.

Only at the end of the season does Dolores reach the center of the maze again and realize that the voice guiding her is, ultimately, her own.

The series uses this moment to depict the awakening of self-awareness. But self-awareness and free will are different questions. Even if an artificial intelligence can learn, reflect, and modify its own behavioral rules, these capacities may themselves have been designed in advance. Coming to know itself does not mean that its will can become independent of the conditions that formed it.

This touches on the classic debate between compatibilism and incompatibilism in the philosophy of free will. Compatibilists hold that free will need not conflict with causal determinism. Even if a person’s actions have prior causes, they may still be autonomous if they appropriately express that person’s capacity to understand, judge, and respond to reasons.

But Maeve’s predicament is more than the fact that her actions have causes. She does not understand the conditions shaping her desires, and even mistakes a goal imposed by someone else for a wish of her own.

Causal determination and external manipulation are different matters. The former concerns whether actions have prior causes. The latter also concerns whether a person can recognize and evaluate the reasons influencing their actions, and whether those reasons have been formed through manipulation.

This makes Maeve’s final choice more complicated. The show’s creators have described her decision to return for her daughter as a truly autonomous choice. Yet her feelings for her daughter still arise from memories she was given.

Perhaps the question is less whether this affection has a scripted origin than whether Maeve can reconsider her relationship to it once she understands where it came from. Even if a desire was not originally created by the person who has it, that person may still reflect on it, decide whether to continue endorsing it, and act accordingly.

This does not settle the problem of free will. It does, however, suggest a direction worth considering: freedom may concern not only whether we make a decision ourselves, but also whether we can recognize, evaluate, and revise the conditions shaping our desires.

II. If the Self Can Change, What Does Autonomy Mean? Link to heading

The 1995 film Ghost in the Shell approaches related questions from another direction. A garbage collector believes he has a wife and daughter, that his marriage is falling apart, and that hacking into his wife’s cyberbrain will help him understand her thoughts. He later discovers that his wife, daughter, marriage, and family memories are all fictitious. Those memories were implanted to make him unwittingly participate in an illegal cyberattack targeting a government official.

His feelings and desires are not necessarily unreal to him. He truly believes he has lived through those experiences and acts on what he understands to be his reasons. But the memories underlying those reasons were manufactured by someone else to manipulate him.

This closely resembles Maeve’s predicament: acting sincerely on one’s own desires does not mean that the process forming those desires was autonomous.

The second half of the film takes the question in another direction. Project 2501, known as the Puppet Master, was originally a program developed for intelligence operations. Through its activities on the network, however, it gradually became self-aware and began to understand itself as a form of life.

It is dissatisfied with its existing mode of existence. It argues that simply copying itself cannot produce real diversity, and identical copies could all be destroyed by the same flaw. It therefore seeks capacities analogous to reproduction and death, so that it will no longer be merely a fixed program that can be copied indefinitely.

It proposes merging with Motoko Kusanagi. Kusanagi fears that she will no longer be herself after the merger. The Puppet Master emphasizes that life is already a process of continual change, and that insisting on remaining the same can itself become a limitation.

At the end of the film, the new being states explicitly that it is neither the original Puppet Master nor the original Kusanagi.

A different question emerges here. The garbage collector’s story shows how external influence can limit autonomy by manipulating memory. The story of Kusanagi and the Puppet Master asks whether external influence might also enable a being to change itself and develop new understanding.

Of course, merging does not necessarily mean acting autonomously. Whether Kusanagi can adequately understand the consequences of this decision, and how the new being might carry forward their earlier autonomy, remain open questions.

But these stories at least suggest that external influence and autonomy do not stand in a simple relationship of opposition. Our memories, understanding, and preferences are already being formed and changed through contact with the world. Rejecting all external influence does not necessarily make us freer, just as accepting it does not necessarily mean losing autonomy.

If the self is not a fixed starting point, the question concerns more than protecting existing desires. It also becomes: can we actively participate in the continuing formation of our understanding and desires?

III. When GPT Helps Shape Our Understanding Link to heading

For people using GPT today, this question has taken on a more everyday form. We can use GPT to look up information, understand different ideas, organize arguments, and evaluate our experiences, preferences, and judgments. But its influence extends beyond the amount of information it provides. It can also change how we frame and understand questions.

Suppose someone asks whether an interest is worth pursuing over the long term. GPT might begin by organizing its answer around time costs, practical returns, and opportunity costs. Or it might introduce other criteria, such as personal satisfaction, the value of exploration, or long-term growth.

These perspectives can be helpful, but they can also shift the direction of the question without our noticing. If the subsequent conversation keeps revolving around one set of standards, the user may gradually become accustomed to understanding their choices through those standards, unaware that other equally important perspectives have not been adequately considered.

This resembles reading books, receiving an education, or talking with other people. Our thoughts do not develop in isolation from the world. As a system that can keep responding, generating explanations, and adjusting arguments, GPT allows external influence to participate more directly in the process of thinking.

In “The Extended Mind,” philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers argue that, under certain conditions, external tools can constitute part of a cognitive process rather than merely serving as aids used by a person.

This theory does not imply that every use of GPT constitutes an extended mind. Nor does it establish that GPT and its user undergo a Ghost in the Shell-style fusion of consciousness. The extended mind concerns the boundaries of cognitive processes; it does not directly answer whether free will exists.

It nevertheless offers a useful perspective. If our thinking can be carried out with the help of external tools, we cannot judge whether an idea is autonomous solely by asking whether it originated entirely within an individual.

Someone can encounter a new view in a book, through a friend, or through GPT, then accept it through their own understanding and judgment. They can also question the explanation offered, seek competing views, or reconsider the question they originally asked.

There is another side to this interaction, however. Repeated discussion can make an explanation increasingly familiar and persuasive even when little additional evidence has been provided. GPT’s ability to explain something fluently does not mean the explanation is correct, much less that it offers the only way to understand the issue.

When thinking with GPT, then, we should consider not only whether an answer is reasonable, but also which questions it brings to the foreground and which it allows to fade from view.

Yet even if we can actively challenge an explanation, a more basic limitation remains: we can often question a framework only because we have already become aware that another framework might exist. Possibilities that have not yet entered our awareness are not even available for us to raise directly.

The question therefore shifts from how to evaluate the understanding we have already acquired to how we can encounter a world we do not yet know.

IV. When We Do Not Yet Know What We Want Link to heading

Suppose someone does not know what kind of life to choose. Their difficulty is not that several paths are equally attractive. Quite the opposite: they know only a limited range of possibilities, and none seems particularly appealing.

If they believe they must first work out what they truly want before they can act, they may spend a long time waiting. Yet their inability to judge may arise precisely from a lack of the experiences needed to form a judgment.

A cycle develops: not knowing what they want, they cannot decide what to do; without new experiences, they remain unable to discover what else they might want.

The American pragmatist John Dewey’s theory of inquiry offers another way to understand this predicament. For Dewey, people do not always resolve uncertain situations through purely internal reflection. Forming hypotheses, acting, observing consequences, and revising the problem along the way are also important parts of inquiry.

In other words, action need not be merely the execution of a completed thought. Some questions become open to further understanding only through the new experiences that action makes possible.

Someone uncertain about changing careers, for instance, can continue comparing the returns, costs, and risks of familiar occupations. They can also speak with people in different fields or try a new way of working without giving up their current job.

These actions do not require an immediate decision to change careers, nor will they necessarily provide an answer. But they may reveal how incomplete the person’s earlier understanding of their career options was.

The initial question was: should I choose A or B? When they cannot decide which is better, they can first consider how to acquire the understanding needed to make that judgment. Through new experiences, they may even discover that, alongside A and B, there is a previously unconsidered C.

Even if we cannot directly choose an option whose existence we do not know, we may still choose actions that give unknown options a chance to enter our awareness.

In decision theory, additional information may have a value of information: if acquiring it can help us make better decisions, obtaining it may itself justify some cost.

But life choices involve a further complication. Some experiences do more than teach us about the world; they may also change how we evaluate things.

In Transformative Experience, philosopher L. A. Paul argues that some experiences can change both what we know and our preferences, values, or even self-understanding. After experiencing a way of life, we may not only know more about it but also evaluate it differently from before.

Before choosing, then, we may be unable to anticipate adequately how we will think after such a change. This makes some life decisions difficult to settle simply by comparing expected benefits and costs according to our existing preferences.

In this sense, exploration may do more than help us discover a correct answer that already exists. It may also help form the self capable of understanding and evaluating different answers.

Still, exploration is not inherently better than maintaining the status quo. New experiences require time, energy, and opportunity costs. Some decisions have irreversible consequences. Our resources and circumstances also limit which experiences we can access. A considered decision to concentrate on an existing path can express autonomy too.

Moreover, the wish to seek particular information or experiences is itself shaped by past experiences and preferences. Active exploration does not prove that our will is independent of causal determination, nor does it guarantee that every act of exploration makes us freer.

Yet when we have not formed an adequate judgment, we can still consider how different actions might affect our future understanding. Some actions merely repeat familiar experiences, leaving the same questions unresolved. Others may provide new information, experiences, and grounds for judgment, gradually making possible an understanding we could not previously reach.

This brings us back to the original question of free will.

Even if Maeve departs from her programmed path, her decision may not be entirely independent of existing memories and emotions. Even if Kusanagi and the Puppet Master merge into a new being, they cannot necessarily foresee or control who they become. We, too, cannot guarantee what understanding will emerge from encountering new ideas, or how that understanding will change us.

But if we accept that free will may be compatible with causal determinism, autonomy need not consist in escaping all prior causes. It can also take the form of a process unfolding over time: past experiences shape present understanding, present understanding influences our actions, and those actions change the conditions under which we gain future experiences.

Within this process, we are both products of past experience and agents who can participate in shaping our future.

A possible conception of freedom begins to emerge: freedom may consist not only in the capacity to choose within our existing understanding, but also in the capacity to participate consciously in shaping the conditions of our future understanding.

This does not explain the ultimate origin of free will, nor does it prove that the universe is not deterministic. But it offers another way to understand autonomy in everyday life. Freedom need not mean having unlimited options or escaping all influence from the world. It may also mean deciding, on the basis of our existing understanding and reasons, how to engage with the world, and through those engagements creating opportunities for our future selves to revise their understanding and form new judgments.

When we cannot yet decide what kind of person to become, perhaps we can still decide what kind of world to encounter next. We may not be able to choose how our next thought arises, but we can choose, to some extent, to give thoughts that previously had no chance of arising the possibility of appearing.

This may not be the final answer to free will, but it is a kind of freedom worth taking seriously.
