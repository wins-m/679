The Other Shore of the Ether

26.10.11 8-minute read
WM
Film • Review

—On Shunji Iwai’s All About Lily Chou-Chou (2001)

People once believed that space was filled with ether: so my physics teacher told us.

So when Lily Chou-Chou’s fans repeatedly discuss the ether on an anonymous forum, even insisting that it exists in music and in the world, I remain at a distance. I do not doubt that music can evoke powerful, real feelings, nor do I object to inventing a language for them. But between “I feel it” and “it exists” lies a threshold I cannot easily cross. Some people on the forum admit that they are not sure whether the ether exists; that, instead, feels understandable to me. As for the solemn declarations, I instinctively raise objections, as I would to an urban legend or testimony about an unidentified flying object.

This keeps me from ever truly entering the inner lives of Yuichi and those fans. Lily Chou-Chou’s music is beautiful, but it cannot protect the people who love it. The same music can belong to both an abuser and a victim, and the apparently sincere understanding found online cannot change the humiliation and harm of the physical world. An affinity in taste, it turns out, is not an affinity in morality. As the film goes on, I find it harder to believe that intimacy wrapped in a shared language can allow us to really know the person on the other side of the screen.

The concert sequence is so long it almost seems to stand still. Yuichi remains outside the venue, his face blank, as though no expression could hold what he now knows. The camera does not explain him for us. Gradually, I stop trying to imagine his shock or pain, and my attention turns to points of light, screens, and the act of watching itself. I even begin thinking about the wave-particle duality of light. At that moment, I realize that I have long since drifted away from the characters’ emotions. I am used to seeking explanations for what I see, used to reacting to every mention of “ether.” Whether this distance comes from reason or from a habit of no longer immersing myself so easily, I do not know.

Yet that does not make the film irrelevant to me. Green fields, white summer uniforms, choirs and orchestras, and the everyday details of school life that need no explanation bring a long-vanished childhood back before my eyes. In primary and middle school, my peers lived in nearby neighborhoods, and we took the same roads home. School was more than a place to study: it was an entire small society that was not easy to leave. There we learned closeness and exclusion, suffered bullying, and sometimes took part in hurting others. Childhood vanishes without a trace, yet follows us like a shadow.

But I believed from an early age that I would eventually leave those people behind. My grades gave me a fairly certain expectation of the future, and changed how I saw the relationships around me. I did not have to fight for a place in every hierarchy among my peers, or attach so much importance to the approval of my present surroundings. In high school, that distance finally became real. I left my hometown and entered an environment that organized almost all of life around studying and getting into university. Each day I would eat quickly, avoid the noisy classroom, go to the reading lounge for a few pages of Dushu, Shouhuo, or Sanwen, and then return to my homework. I did not find this solitude difficult to explain. I understood my choice, and understood why no one came along, though sometimes I still wished I had someone to read these things with.

At the time, I had not seriously imagined that university would necessarily bring people of my own kind. Still, I vaguely assumed that a better school would offer a richer, more independent intellectual life. Only later did I gradually discover that grades, schools, and academic disciplines do not necessarily select for curiosity about knowledge, much less for shared aesthetic tastes or emotional understanding. A person can keep reaching places thought worth pursuing, yet never know why they should stay. The standards that had once allowed me to leave were now beginning to prescribe new destinations for me.

Lily Chou-Chou makes me think again about what this educational order, which I later came to question, had once given me. An extreme form of exam-oriented education compressed the world into grades, courses, and university admissions. It also gave some of those within it a temporary freedom to refuse other power games. It could not eliminate bullying among classmates, and certainly could not protect everyone, but it did at least offer a relatively clear route out. Beyond the school gates, the criteria for judgment were no longer so clear, and opportunities were no longer determined solely by exams one could complete independently. That brief shelter disappeared.

Yuichi has no such credible path. Classes continue, teachers continue performing their duties, and adults continue running their own lives. The formal order appears uninterrupted, yet the violent order established by Hoshino functions just as smoothly. When theft is discovered, the incident is handled according to established procedures; why a student has ended up in such a situation seems no longer to be the more pressing question. Adults are not absent. Some even participate directly in exploiting children. What is truly missing is the judgment and action needed to notice harm beyond the rules, and, when necessary, resist existing power. Standing by is not the opposite of violence; it can be the environment that allows violence to continue.

Yet Shunji Iwai films this world with such brightness and clarity. Rice fields ripple in the wind, white uniforms catch the sunlight, and the music is so light it seems almost transparent. Beauty does not stop violence. It seems instead like a surface that remains intact, accommodating even what should be intolerable. A kite rises into the sky, briefly tracing the shape of freedom, then falls. The shot of Yuichi standing on a chair, and the equipment surrounding his head as his hair is dyed, also make me think again and again of death within ordinary gestures. Not every shot announces that a character intends to die. Rather, death seems always to lurk within the apparent stability of everyday life.

This brings A Brighter Summer Day to mind. There, too, are hierarchies, obedience, humiliation, and harm among adolescents. But Edward Yang continually connects personal catastrophe back to migration, war, political fear, and a history from which there is no escape. Lily Chou-Chou seems to push such explanations beyond the frame, leaving only an orderly world and the violence within it, for which it is hard to know whom to hold to account. The difference between the films certainly does not amount to the entire difference between two societies. The adolescent world in Japan also has a historical background of economic stagnation and social change. Iwai simply chooses to let those things sink more deeply into atmosphere and aesthetics, rather than become clues we can follow.

Perhaps that is why I cannot see these images merely as the pain of youth. Across the strait and across the years, my first thought is still of the “us” in A Brighter Summer Day, of older wars, separations, and historical ruptures. A habit of obedience formed by history does not automatically disappear because everyday life is tidy. But I also know that the aesthetics of a single film cannot justify a complete explanation of a society or a people. What those grand histories ultimately leave behind is sometimes only a tiny trace of childhood: white summer uniforms, and the creases in a red scarf.

I also remember something from high school. I often went alone to read in a building where there were few people, passing an out-of-the-way restroom on the way. Once, as I came out, I met a school caretaker who did odd jobs and had difficulty speaking. He had brought some fruit from the staff cafeteria, and when he saw me, he insisted on putting it into my hands. I was surprised and touched. Later, I kept wondering whether he had seen this student, always alone in quiet corners, as a lonely child who needed looking after. My loneliness did matter to me, but I also believed in the life I had chosen. The two did not conflict.

Perhaps he had thought nothing of the sort, and simply wanted to share a piece of fruit. That possibility does not diminish the meaning of the encounter. Someone with no obligation to look after me had taken notice of me and offered a little kindness that asked for nothing in return. Years later, it forms a strange contrast with the ether whose existence is repeatedly affirmed on the forum. Music can give feelings a home, but it does not take on anyone’s responsibility to protect another person. Institutions can offer people a way out, but they do not automatically produce care. And a kindness without a name is sometimes simply one person handing fruit to another.

I still do not believe in the ether, and I cannot know where the paths I never took would have led me. I once worked hard to leave; after leaving, I began to question the direction that path had prescribed. Watching freedom appear and disappear again and again in the film, I remembered the small measure of freedom to leave that I had once possessed. Those who left, those who stayed, the orders and shadows of childhood, and those small acts of care that cannot be proved but did happen. Perhaps this education really was a kind of grace.
