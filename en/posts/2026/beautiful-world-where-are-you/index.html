Beautiful World, Where Are You?

26.9.20 8-minute read
WM
Film • 5 Centimeters per Second • Memory

30-second summary: After watching the live-action 5 Centimeters per Second, I remembered Sally Rooney’s Beautiful World, Where Are You — a novel I definitely read and have almost entirely forgotten. With soft light, casting, and a handful of slices of growing up, the film summons childhood memories that have no clear object. But it never explains why that beautiful world disappeared. Perhaps images do not preserve the past; perhaps they briefly let us feel it again inside reality.

This is the first pass, closer to film analysis. A second pass, written the next day and closer to personal reflection, is here.

Read, but not remembered Link to heading

Beautiful World, Where Are You is Sally Rooney’s third novel. I recently downloaded her fourth. But I have no memory at all of what the third one was about. Three or four young people, perhaps. All Rooney novels seem to be that way.

But I must have read this one: during some definite stretch of time, after seeing its blue cover in a foreign-language bookshop, and the large black capitals on it — Beautiful World, Where Are You.

It is like 5 Centimeters per Second. I must have watched the animation. I do not remember its plot either; I cannot even find a record of it on Douban. It seems to have sunk into my private memory along with some lost period of life — lost so completely that I cannot even say which period it was.

Two full hours of soft light made me feel as if images were the medium of memory itself: two frames can call resonance back into a viewer.

Perhaps that is only because I remember in light and shadow rather than in pictures. I may have watched the animated 5 Centimeters per Second at twenty, yet not have felt anything as particular as I did watching this live-action film. And how long will these feelings remain? The protagonists’ promise in primary school can still be remembered at thirty — though the two protagonists adopt two opposite ways of remembering. Ten minutes after the end credits, how could I declare that this film will stay with me for any particular length of time?

Where did that beautiful world go? Link to heading

Halfway through the film, a sentence came to me: Beautiful World, Where Are You?

It came from the soft lighting throughout, and from the faces of children in its primary-school and high-school years — I remembered Tanegashima, but had never realized it came from 5 Centimeters per Second. We have long forgotten primary school and everything earlier. Yet it is like a minor character’s description of a film at a bookshop table: they do not remember why they were moved to tears, only that, when they encounter the smell of that cinema again, olfactory memory reactivates an emotion with no object.

Almost all of us have lost the visual or graphic memories of childhood. The live-action 5 Centimeters per Second, however, uses soft light and precise casting to awaken a shared bodily sense of it.

That is where the question comes from: where did such a beautiful childhood, such a beautiful world, go?

I had forgotten the animation’s plot, but forgetting it meant I still remembered its kind of narrative. As I watched, I kept trying to work out what kind it was. Then I remembered Peppermint Candy: “Life is beautiful, isn’t it?” If that film tells how a protagonist is destroyed step by step by growing up, then what exactly is the ending of 5 Centimeters per Second?

As the train moves through the snow, the sole middle-school sequence gradually passed from my memory of the animation into a tangible setting on the screen. My question became sharper. By then the film had covered two-thirds of its length, yet no environmental factor had appeared that could explain the protagonist’s present state. What, then, causes the familiar Shinkai-style cutting ending — the female protagonist’s eventual life as an impassive work machine?

Beautiful worlds exist only in scenes from the past: childhood and school. Their opposite is never described. Precisely because even that opposite has not appeared in the present, the Beautiful World of the present has disappeared without trace. Or rather: viewers may be moved by the synaesthetic memory of childhood, while unable to say why that beautiful childhood was lost in the present — which, for viewers themselves, means lost in reality.

Anyone can of course supply a plausible explanation, but the film itself does not explain its protagonist’s transformation. To me, moving house and changing schools serve the narrative and the making of the characters’ bond, rather than any critique of minors’ rights the film never makes.

The film simply stops. In the same seat at the planetarium, the two protagonists make opposite choices: one is an answer; the other is a question put to oneself.

I do not remember this planetarium scene from the animation. But it unmistakably supplies an answer in advance to the celebrated pilgrimage location and to the title’s “five centimeters per second.”

Two realities Link to heading

So the film’s egg-and-chicken relation turns entirely inward, toward character. The role of “environment” is never stated. Or perhaps, because the environment is nearly identical for the two protagonists — their experiences merely more dramatic and extreme than the viewers’ — what had been the effect of inner disposition and change becomes the cause of a choice made in the present, in 2009.

Having deeply absorbed the other’s influence and made it the core of her life, the first protagonist alone has settled on her answer more than ten years in advance. It is a fundamental answer, enough to produce an immediate, END-like decision to relocate. After years in Tokyo, it is hard not to connect that act to the poster picked up at the planetarium.

My remembered Peppermint Candy-like narrative, and perhaps the reason I forgot the earlier animation — a tragic destiny of growing into someone one hates — were really built on my impression of Shinkai animation as a story with one protagonist. After their separation in primary school and the snowy middle-school night, the second protagonist moves to Tanegashima and is more likely to inhabit a state of loss. He seems not merely to have failed to internalize the other’s influence, but to have lost the integrity of himself. The rocket he “encounters” in Tanegashima lifts off; perhaps the author assumes that readers and viewers will accept this helpless process of growing up.

I cannot know what the film’s unspoken “environment” is. Is it some structure of regional economics in which “returning to Tokyo” consumes a life?

From the perspective of two protagonists, the writer and director seem to offer a glib admonition: there is no such thing as a Beautiful World, only one “reality”; one may choose the only reality that is Beautiful, or the only reality waiting to become Beautiful.

Yet the film’s very release and screening communicate another, opposite meaning. It is precisely because a Beautiful World does not exist in reality that images, animation, and language are needed to awaken feelings about childhood, growing up, and friendship — feelings we do not always possess in the real world, and sometimes have no way even to possess or find.

If it really existed all around us, as the first protagonist seems to have made it, why would two hours of soft-lit images be needed to place fairy-tale slices of growing up on a screen? And in my view, the ending does exactly the opposite: with two lies of life — neither containing an answer the creators truly endorse — it deliberately hands the viewer a blade.

A part of reality Link to heading

Leaving the film itself aside, I had not watched films seriously for a long time. There was once a period when cinema was where Beautiful World lived for me. I can roughly see why I watched 5 Centimeters per Second but did not properly record it on Douban: it resembles the conviction I had in that period.

Like the tragic ending I remembered from the animated 5 Centimeters per Second, the outcome of growing up was the same as the second protagonist’s gradual destruction. The first protagonist simply does not collapse in the way the second one does. In other parts of life — or under another ordering of values — she will still have dissatisfactions. The film’s details suggest as much, especially where conversations stop short: details the animation may well have been unable to make.

Perhaps, on the question of growing up, the first protagonist is honest with life and with the reality she inhabits. But is being honest with life equivalent to being honest with oneself? We probably have to return to the observer’s position: to watching and seeing. What the screen presents is the variety of lives, states, and human circumstances. At times it lets a viewer identify with or feel for a character so completely that they enter themselves into the story. That does not mean the character’s experience must be universal, or repeated in everyone else’s growth.

Then Beautiful World exists on the screen, or in the first two-thirds of a film. That remains a reality I can experience, and part of Beautiful World. While watching, I recalled my own childhood and school years. I cannot map my experience onto the characters in 5 Centimeters per Second, but those familiar synaesthetic memories are real.

Why not make watching a film a part of oneself, then?

From another angle, “being unable to map it” seems to correspond to the second protagonist’s state of loss. Not having is, in substance, losing; both are forms of helplessness. That may be what 5 Centimeters per Second gives me. Whether Beautiful World vanished or never arrived, it is only a promise worth remembering, not one that must be sought out or fulfilled.

God is dead. Carry the dead God with you, and walk in a Beautiful World that does not lead to Beautiful World.
