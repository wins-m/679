Do We Need More Films, or Better Ways of Watching Them?

26.9.25 4-minute read
WM
Film • Music • Recommender Systems • Attention

A question has been on my mind lately: why has there never been a film recommendation system that truly satisfies me? This post is a draft of an idea, not yet anything like a product design.

As a long-time Douban user and a fairly seasoned film lover, I’m used to finding films there. The usual path goes like this: under a film, look at “people who liked this also liked”, or browse “similar films”, then keep branching out by rating, director, cast, and tags. It works, of course, but it always seems to stay inside a fairly simple network of relations: I like A, so give me B; people who like A also like C; A is similar to D, so recommend D.

Music, meanwhile, seems to have made recommendation into something much more mature.

1. NetEase Cloud Music showed me a remarkably powerful way to recommend Link to heading

For the past few years I’ve used NetEase Cloud Music. It generates recommended playlists for me every day, and even if one of them misses my taste on a given day, another usually keeps me listening. One mode fits my habits especially well: it starts from songs I’ve explicitly marked as liked, and looks for similar music. So I keep hearing new songs. The mechanism genuinely works.

But after a few years, I noticed something interesting. My “likes” kept growing, my saved songs kept growing, my playlists kept getting longer, yet the number of songs I actually remembered did not grow at the same pace. Many songs I’d heard only once or twice. I didn’t even know what they were called or which album they came from. I had simply thought one was “pretty good” at some point, saved it, and moved on to the next.

The recommender succeeded brilliantly at helping me discover more music, but it never made sure I actually came to know that music.

2. What actually changed me was stopping recommendations Link to heading

My NetEase membership was about to expire recently, so I downloaded all the music and playlists I’d liked (post in Chinese) and switched to a local player. That, of all things, made me listen to music seriously again for the first time in a long while.

I stopped constantly looking for the next track. Sometimes I’d just open one artist, sometimes listen to a single album, sometimes play an album I’d never really listened to from start to finish. And I found that I had already encountered so much music I had never truly gotten to know. Some songs the recommender had pushed to me before; I’d heard them and saved them, but only now did I notice for the first time: so this song belongs to this album; so this artist has work like this too; so what I really love on this album is actually a few other tracks.

That’s an entirely different experience from “discovering new songs”. It’s more like rediscovering what I already have.

3. Listening itself may need attention Link to heading

Around the same time, I started logging the music I listen to, the way I log films and books on Douban. Music used to be mostly background — something on while working, walking, exercising, doing something else. Now I’ve begun to feel that some music is simply meant to be listened to with attention.

Recently my headphones broke, so I’ve been exercising without music. At first it felt like something was missing, but then I realised that exercise is already a complete experience in itself: body, breath, road, wind, ambient sound. Music sometimes pulls attention away from the body and the present. I started to wonder: are we using music to have an experience, or to escape the present?

Of course the two aren’t necessarily in conflict, and I don’t conclude from this that music recommendation is bad. It just struck me that a stronger recommender doesn’t necessarily mean a better experience.

4. This made me rethink film recommendation Link to heading

Films and music differ in one very important way: a song may last a few minutes, while a film usually takes an hour and a half or longer. Watching films is therefore itself a limited resource of attention.

If a recommender gets better and better at telling me “you’ve finished A, so B is what you’ll most likely enjoy next”, it can certainly keep me discovering films. But it can also produce a strange state: I’m always looking for the next film, without ever really entering the ones I’ve already seen.

That stands in contrast to how I’ve been experiencing films lately. After watching 5 Centimeters per Second recently, I didn’t simply go looking for something “similar”. Instead I started asking: why did this film make me feel this way? What’s its relationship to the novel? What does it have to do with my own past? Why did certain scenes hold me for so long?

The clearest thing was a feeling of wanting to stay just five more minutes. After the film, I kept wondering why, knowing full well that some things are bound to end, I still wanted them to last a little longer — and that later connected with something I’ve been thinking about for the past few years: how to keep on living. Then I read the novel, and found that things which flash by in the film, things I hadn’t even registered, are written out much more concretely there; so I started looking again at what I’d taken to be mere scenery in the film. Further on still, it led me to Beautiful World, Where Are You, and to other works about how people miss each other, and how they go on living.

A film stops being just a node. It becomes an entrance.

5. What I really want may not be “smarter film recommendation” Link to heading

If I ever actually build a film recommendation site, I don’t want it to be just “Douban plus a better algorithm”. That would still be answering “what should you watch next?”, whereas the question I’m really interested in now might be:

How can we help a person build a longer, deeper relationship with films?

The two questions are very close, yet they could lead to entirely different products.

6. Film recommendation should have at least two modes Link to heading

Discovery Link to heading

This is the recommender we all know: you like A, so you might like B. Its goal is to let me meet things I’ve never met before. That’s still enormously valuable.

Deepening Link to heading

The other kind is entirely different. It’s in no hurry to push me towards something new; instead it keeps digging into what I’ve already seen. For example:

You gave this film 4 stars three years ago, and have never watched it again.

You’ve watched a lot of director X’s work recently, but none of their early films.

You love this film, but haven’t seen another work from the same period that’s closely connected to it.

You’ve saved 8 films by this director, but have actually watched only 2.

The goal of this kind of recommendation isn’t to raise consumption. It’s to help me get to know the films I’ve already met.

Take it one step further and it could even become an “anti-algorithmic” design. A good recommender shouldn’t necessarily always chase the next thing most likely to satisfy me; sometimes it should deliberately break that momentum:

You’ve watched five similar films in a row. No similar films next time.

You saved 200 films in the past year, but have actually watched only 30. No new saves today.

Three years ago you loved this film. Back then you hadn’t yet seen all these later works. Watch it again now, and you might see something different.

You’ve watched a lot of new films lately. Today there’s nothing to look for — pick one at random from the films you’ve loved before.

It’s not maximising content consumption. It’s protecting attention.

7. “Recommendation” needn’t even be only about films Link to heading

A single film can lead to many things:

film → director → other works
film → actor → films of a completely different kind
film → novel → writer
film → cinematography → artist
film → music → album
film → era → history
film → theme → a completely different work

So a truly interesting film system might not be a Movie Recommender at all, but something more like a Personal Film Map. What it records isn’t “what should this person watch?” but “where has this person already been?”, and “which places are worth going back to?”

8. What should film recommendation actually optimise for? Link to heading

A conventional recommender might care about:

  • click-through rate
  • play rate
  • completion rate
  • save rate
  • time spent
  • recommendation accuracy

But if I really built a system like this, I’d rather know a different set of measures:

  • Did I actually remember it after watching?
  • Did I ever rewatch it?
  • Did one film lead me to learn about another work?
  • Did it form a new path of interest?
  • Did one film bring me into a new director, country, era, or art form?
  • Did it reduce the meaningless “next one”?
  • Can a single viewing still produce new meaning years later?

There could even be a rather odd measure: whether a film can still find its way back into a person’s life years later. If the answer is yes, then the film may truly have become that person’s own.

9. Is this site worth building at all? Link to heading

I don’t know yet. Maybe it isn’t worth building at all; maybe Douban is already good enough; maybe the real issue isn’t the algorithm but that the way I watch films has changed. Maybe this idea will turn out to be just a line of thought, never a product.

Still, I think it’s worth writing down first, because for the first time I see fairly clearly that what I really want to solve may not be “film recommendation isn’t smart enough”, but rather:

Once a person has more and more films, music, books, and other cultural works, how do they avoid endlessly consuming new things without ever truly owning any of them?

If that’s the case, the film recommender is only an entrance. The larger problem it’s ultimately trying to solve is how to help a person make better use of their own attention.

So for now, I’m in no hurry to design a database, an algorithm, or a website interface. I’ll keep watching films, keep logging, keep listening to music, and keep observing how I actually discover, watch, forget, and rediscover a work. If after a while this question is still there, maybe then I’ll start designing it.

When that time comes, what I hope to design isn’t a site that tells me “what to watch next”, but one that can occasionally tell me:

You’ve already come this far.
Here are a few things you once passed by, but haven’t yet truly seen.
