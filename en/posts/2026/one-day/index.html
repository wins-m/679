If There Are Hundreds of Thousands of Todays Left

26.10.4 8-minute read
WM
Film • Television • Review

I watched the film version of One Day in November 2022. I can barely remember how I watched it or what I thought at the time. I remember that its chronology felt different from the series: the first one day, I think, was not fully explained until the very end. Somewhere in the middle, I realized that each installment showed the same date in a different year. By the time the story returned to where it began, Emma was dead. The film also spent less time on how Emma had loved Dexter since university, and how that love persisted for years in all kinds of forms. After watching the series, I realized that, at least in the story as it is told, she never found anyone she loved more than Dexter.

What stayed with me most from the film was not the romance itself. I remember Emma saying something like I still … but I cannot …, though I have long forgotten the exact words. I also remember Dexter’s father telling him, after Emma’s death, to pretend she was still there: “Do you know how I got through the last twenty years?” After watching the film in 2022, I thought of One Day less as a love story than as a story about learning to lay the dead to rest inside yourself. I went looking for the film’s theme, We Had Today. What it left me with then was simple: to live in the face of death. What we have now does not last just because it matters. If there is something you want to do today, you may never get another chance.

After finishing all fourteen episodes of the series this time, I felt much less moved. Perhaps when each year gets its own episode, death no longer takes up the most space in the story. Ordinary life does: graduating, looking for work, temp jobs, bills, drinking, falling in love, breaking up, changing jobs, parents getting sick, marriage, divorce, having children, writing, publishing, buying a house. When Emma is young, she has an almost unreal fervor: romantic idealism, firm political opinions, a belief that she should write and that her life should amount to something. Dexter has a different kind of dreaminess. He has no idea what to do, so he travels, parties, drinks, then drifts into television, pushed along by the spotlight, fame, and the people around him. They seem completely different, but both are still living inside a dream.

The dream does not shatter on any one particular day. Life simply grows more concrete. Emma goes from restaurant waitress to teacher, from aspiring writer to full-time novelist. Dexter goes from having no idea what to do, to becoming a television presenter, to falling out of the spotlight. All the while there are bills, work, relationships, and every kind of petty trouble. Watching twenty years unfold in one stretch left me with a strong impression: when we are young, we often think that one day a fundamentally different life will begin. For now, we are preparing, waiting, lost, making our way there. We wait until we find work we like, until we have money, until we move to another city, until we become the person we want to be. That will be the start of the “new life.” But after One Day lays out twenty years, one year at a time, there seems to be no such moment. Even after a dream comes true, life goes on. Emma really does become a writer. And then? She keeps living, one day at a time.

What seems to move these lives forward is less an individual pursuit than people, and the changing relationships between them: Emma’s university roommate, Dexter’s parents, his later marriage and daughter, and the two of them drawing close and drifting apart over twenty years. A new life rarely seems to belong to one person alone. If you took away all these relationships and left only hobbies, dreams, or a career, those things suddenly seem too thin to carry someone through decades of this mundane world. Dexter makes this especially clear. For a long time he has no particular direction, yet people who love him, help him, or use him keep pushing him along, and somehow he stumbles into the next year.

When I started the second episode, Emma had just graduated and was visiting Dexter’s home for the first time. My first thought was still: how lucky to have a friend across class lines. Emma is poor and has no idea where to go after graduation; Dexter lives in a completely different world. He has money, a loving family, parents who care for him, and an education most people could never access. As I watched more of Dexter’s life, I began to feel a certain sadness. The year before his mother dies, she visits him in southern Europe and puts it plainly: he has the best money and education can offer, yet he keeps choosing not to choose. Or perhaps he can afford to waste his life. The choices other people struggle desperately to obtain have been there for him from the start, but that has not helped him know what to choose. Money, education, and family can give him possibilities and catch him when he falls, again and again. They cannot tell him why he should choose any one life. The ability to choose does not automatically give you a reason to choose.

This feeling also connects, in some way, to my own sense of nihilism. This morning I had a long conversation with an alumnus and fellow professional quite a bit older than me. He talked about the social value of our industry, then brought up DeepSeek and showed me a chip company he had helped found. I used to get stuck on questions like, “Why am I not doing better in this field?” But perhaps the question is not only whether I can do the work well. Even if I do it well, how much can the work itself offer in the way of a sense of purpose that truly drives me? Dexter has so many choices and still does not know why to make any of them. Ability, opportunity, and meaning are not the same thing.

Of course, One Day is still a fairly conventional story. In British fiction, it seems there is always some young English major who ends up succeeding as a writer. Trinity, Edinburgh, or the “good friend” from the local comprehensive who has gone off to America. Emma happens to be discovered by the publishing industry and becomes a successful novelist. Dexter happens to be lifted up by the spotlight, then destroyed by it; his career and marriage happen to go wrong. Many people enter their lives, yet somehow no one ever quite replaces the other. After twenty years of missed connections, they finally find their way to each other, and then Emma happens to be hit by a car. These are all choices the author made. If Emma had met someone who understood and respected her more, someone she liked more than Dexter; if Dexter’s marriage had been happy; if the two of them had simply become old friends who occasionally liked each other’s posts a few years after graduation, there would be no story.

Real life does not guarantee that the most important person in your twenties will still be the most important person in your forties. So the ordinary details I described earlier do not become universal truths just because One Day puts them on screen. Emma becoming a writer is not universal experience. Neither is Dexter’s fall from television stardom, and neither, certainly, is a fatal accident. Many things in the series that look like “life” are really devices that keep these two people central to each other across twenty years. Once those devices are removed, I am not sure how much of the story remains that speaks to ordinary life.

Interestingly, I find the first few episodes more real. We in East Asia probably do not spend our twenties quite like this, but graduating, feeling lost, getting a first job and finding it nothing like you imagined, not knowing where you will go next, and watching people who used to be close drift into entirely different lives — at least I know what those things feel like. I do not know what comes in the years after. What marriage is like, what divorce is like, what it means to become a parent, how you see your twenty-five-year-old self at thirty-five, what it means to have known someone for twenty years, how to go on after someone important to you actually dies: for now, stories are the only way I can imagine these things. Perhaps it is simply because I have only lived up to this age.

So when I watched the film in 2022, I understood it through its ending: people die, so treasure today; if there is something you want to do now, perhaps you will not get another chance. Four years later, after watching the series again, I find myself asking a different question from inside the texture of ordinary life: if someone has hundreds of thousands of todays ahead of them, what makes those days worth living, one after another?

One Day does not answer that question. Or perhaps its answer is still too conventional: love, relationships, memories, and the days once shared with someone. A career does not answer it. Money does not answer it. Success does not answer it. The grand ideas we believed in when we were young seem not to answer it either. What remains is something very small, like the words in the title: one day, and then another one day, until one year there is no next time.
