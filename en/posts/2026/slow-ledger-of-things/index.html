The Slow Ledger of Things I Own

26.9.19 9-minute read
WM
Life • Consumption • Data • Personal Projects

30-second summary: A bone-conduction headset died after almost three years, and I wanted to know whether it had been worth buying. That question exposed a gap: I had never systematically recorded any durable thing I owned. So I built a small site that starts with an Excel sheet and derives service life, daily cost, cost per use, and retirement reasons, then turns the collection into a timeline. The public record now contains 69 items, with another 4 deliberately hidden; the items still in service add up to about ¥70.41 per day on today’s amortization basis. The number does not tell me what I should buy. It makes me see that an object’s value is made in all the ordinary days after it is bought.

I recently made a small website: Gear Log.

It is not a collection catalogue or a personal-finance dashboard. It records durable things I have owned: headphones, phones, computers, shoes, backpacks, razors, cameras, furniture, kitchen gear. Each item has an acquisition date, price, rough use frequency, and—when the time comes—a retirement date and reason. The site draws their service lives as a timeline and calculates their daily cost and estimated cost per use.

It began with something very small.

On September 12, my Shokz OpenRun Pro bone-conduction headset finally failed after nearly three years. A broken headset usually produces one next step: look for a replacement. But I paused on a different question: had it actually been worth it?

That sounds like a simple division problem. Take the purchase price and divide it by the days used. In practice, it was not. How long had I really had it? How often had I used it each week? Was its failure evidence of poor quality, or the natural end of an object that had done its job? What if something was later sold or given away? More awkwardly, I realized I could not answer those questions for most of my things. I remembered buying them; I rarely remembered the life they had gone on to have.

The project gradually grew from a spreadsheet into a slow ledger for objects.

Not “Was It Worth Buying?” but “How Long Did It Stay?” Link to heading

We tend to judge a purchase by the moment it is made.

Buy too high, regret it. See a discount later, regret it. Discover a better model, regret it. Consumption can feel like an exam that ends at checkout, as if the answer were fixed the instant payment goes through.

Durable things do not work that way.

A computer has no demonstrated value on the day it arrives. It earns its place through hundreds of workdays, trips, and last-minute moments when it happens to be there. A pair of shoes is not valuable merely because it was cheap; it becomes valuable by walking a lot of unremarkable roads with you. The cost of an object only becomes legible in time.

So the first field I calculate for each item is not its price but its days in service: both the acquisition day and retirement day count, and an item still in use keeps counting through today. Net cost is purchase price minus resale or recovery value. Divide that by days in service, and you get daily cost.

The formula is plainly crude. It cannot measure joy, utility, or the value of an object that saved the day at exactly the right moment. Use frequency is usually an estimate, not surveillance. But it gives me a perspective I did not have before: instead of only asking what something cost at the time, I can ask how long it remained in my life and what kind of use it received.

The longest-serving public item is still a Sony RX100M3 bought in 2015: as of September 19, it has been around for 4,121 days—more than eleven years. It is no longer my most-used device and often sits in a drawer. But it still comes out for travel, light packing, or strong sunlight, when a phone is not the right tool. Its daily cost is about ¥1.05.

Another item makes an equally useful distinction. I bought a ThinkPad T480s in 2018. A newer MacBook replaced it as my main machine, but the ThinkPad did not really leave. It got memory, storage, and network upgrades; it ran several operating systems; eventually it became a small Docker server. The record says it has served 2,970 days. More interestingly, it made me notice that losing an item’s primary role is not the same as retiring it.

Objects do not always travel in a straight line from new to dead. Some are demoted, some are repurposed, and some become useful again in a narrow situation.

I Did Not End Up Building a Budgeting App Link to heading

At first, this project could easily have grown into a complicated consumption-management tool: every price, receipt, field, and use logged in detail.

I chose not to build that.

The sole manual source is now an Excel workbook. Categories, use periods, retirement reasons, and the hide-this-item switch are dropdowns. I enter a name, acquisition date, price, and approximate use frequency; scripts derive IDs, service days, daily cost, cost per use, payback date, and collection-wide summaries.

This is not because Excel is especially elegant. It is because the friction of recording matters. A project that might gain or retire only a handful of things each year should not require me to memorize English enums or open a complicated web form just to enter a razor. A system that survives is often not the one with the most features; it is the one that does not feel burdensome when I want to add the next row.

The page itself does only a few things: a summary at the top; horizontal bars for each item’s service life; a sortable and filterable list; and, on an item’s detail view, a curve showing its cost fall with time. There is also a chart for the daily cost of all active items: it steps up when something enters the collection and down when something retires.

I especially like that chart because it turns consumption from isolated orders into a continuing environment.

What Is in the Ledger So Far Link to heading

As of September 19, 2026, the public portion contains 69 items, with another 4 whole records encrypted and hidden. Of the public items, 53 remain in use and 16 have retired.

The public entries add up to roughly ¥79,033 purchased, ¥2,076 recovered through resale, and ¥76,958 in net cost. The items still in service total about ¥70.41 per day when each is amortized over its service life through today.

That last figure needs a warning: ¥70.41 is not money actually leaving my bank account every day. It is a snapshot created by spreading every active item’s net cost over the days it has served so far. A recently acquired expensive item pushes it up; time naturally pushes it down. It is not a KPI I should try to minimize.

It is closer to a photograph: if the durable things around me are treated as depreciating pieces of everyday infrastructure, what do they collectively cost at this moment?

Electronics dominate that photograph. There are 37 public entries in that category; they account for most public net spending and roughly ¥64.24 of the daily total. That is not surprising: computers, phones, cameras, and cycling equipment are expensive and overlap with work, communication, recording, and leisure. Appliances, kitchen gear, and accessories are less conspicuous, much cheaper in total, and often serve for a long time.

The median service life across all public items is 727 days—about two years. Among the 16 retired items, the most common reason is not breakage but idleness: 5 items. Breakage and resale account for 4 each, consumable exhaustion for 2, and loss for 1.

That is more specific than asking whether I buy too much. Many things do not leave because they break; the context that justified them disappears, or a better replacement appears. Idleness is not a moral failure and does not automatically mean waste. It is a reminder that the future use case imagined at purchase may not actually arrive.

Some objects have near-zero or negative daily cost because they were resold or recovered. Some new devices look expensive per day only because not enough time has passed to spread the cost. The data puts them on the same chart without pretending to issue a verdict.

Hiding the Object, Not Just the Price Link to heading

The project also has a slightly counterintuitive privacy decision: encryption does not redact a price. It removes an entire item from the public page.

I eventually realized that what I might not want public is not “how much it cost,” but “that this object ever existed.” A hidden record appears in no public list, timeline, ranking, or aggregate; the public page only discloses the number of hidden items. Entering the password decrypts those records and adds them back to the complete statistics.

This is not a serious security boundary. The ciphertext and decryption code are both present in a static page, and a weak password can still be tried offline. It is simply an in-between layer for “I want a complete record, but I do not want every trace of my life laid out in public.”

I like that boundary. Self-quantification often carries an unspoken urge: once something is recorded, it should all be public, comparable, and optimized. But the first purpose of a record is to understand one’s own life, not turn it into a dashboard for display.

A Slow Ledger Cannot Make My Decisions for Me Link to heading

Finishing this has not made me better at deciding whether to buy something.

An item with low daily cost is not necessarily better than one with high daily cost. Leave a rarely used object around long enough and its daily cost will always fall; that is why the site keeps cost per use and frequency alongside it. Conversely, a newly bought computer may look expensive per day without being a failed purchase. It simply has not yet lived enough days.

The useful part is not stamping each object “worth it” or “not worth it.” It is leaving behind a fact that can be revisited: why I bought it, what it later did, and why it eventually left.

That may be what is interesting about durable things. Unlike a one-off purchase, they do not end at payment; they are repeatedly justified in ordinary days. They may accompany commuting, work, travel, moving, and reading, or silently settle into a drawer. When they finally break, are sold, or are given away, you suddenly realize how long they had been there.

The ledger is still new and incomplete. But it has already made me notice things I would otherwise forget: a charging cable, a pair of shoes, an old computer, a headset that finally stopped working.

I am not trying to find a defense for every purchase.

I just do not want things that spent part of my life with me to leave with nothing more than: “Oh, it broke. Buy another one.”
